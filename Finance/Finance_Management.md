# Financial Management — Complete Notes

## The Role of Financial Management

Financial management is the **planning, obtaining, and managing of funds** to achieve business objectives. It supports all other business functions (operations, marketing, HR) and is critical for profitability, growth, and survival.

**Key financial objectives:**
- **Profitability** — maximising earnings after expenses
- **Liquidity** — ability to pay short-term debts as they fall due
- **Solvency** — ability to pay both short-term AND long-term debts
- **Growth** — increasing the size/value of the business over time
- **Efficiency** — using resources effectively to maintain stability and profit

---

## Financial Planning

Financial management is responsible for financial planning in both the **short term** and **long term**.

**Financial planning includes:**
1. Determining the viability of projects
2. Making decisions and projections for future liquidity and performance
3. Setting financial goals and determining/implementing strategies to achieve them

**Financial needs:**
- **New business** → determine establishment costs → finance through debt and/or equity
- **Existing business** → determine financing required for future plans

**Financial control** ensures plans are implemented effectively:
1. Monitoring and controlling budgets (ongoing throughout the year)
2. Preparing financial statements (cash flow, income statement, balance sheet — usually monthly)
3. Using financial ratios to monitor financial performance

## Financial Institutions

Financial institutions are intermediaries that channel funds between savers/lenders and borrowers.

### Investment Banks
- Trade in money, securities and financial futures
- Arrange **long-term finance** for company expansion
- Advise clients on foreign exchange cover
- Advise on **mergers** (two businesses agree to merge, e.g. Westpac and St George) and **takeovers** (one business buys another, the other ceases to exist)
- Operate **unit trusts** — set up for wealthy people to minimise tax, paid out to beneficiaries regularly
- Arrange **overseas finance** — businesses borrow from overseas banks at cheaper interest rates (even after fees, still cheaper than borrowing domestically)
- **Example:** Macquarie Bank

### Finance and Life Insurance Companies
- Non-bank financial intermediaries specialising in smaller commercial finance
- Provide **unsecured and secured loans** to businesses and individuals
- People pay insurance premiums → premiums are lent out to businesses → company earns income from interest payments

### Superannuation Funds
- Provide funds to the corporate sector through investment funds received from superannuation contributions

### Unit Trusts (Mutual Funds)
- Take funds from a large number of small investors
- Invest them in specific types of financial assets

### Australian Securities Exchange (ASX)
- Functions as a **market operator, clearing house and payment system facilitator**
- Oversees compliance with operating rules
- Promotes standards of **corporate governance** among listed companies

**ASX acts as two markets:**

| Market | How it works | Who gets the money |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Primary Market** | New shares are issued and sold for the first time | The company receives the money |
| **Secondary Market** | Pre-existing shares are traded between investors | The original shareholder (seller) receives the money — the company gets nothing |

> **Exam tip:** IPO (Initial Public Offering) happens on the primary market. Day-to-day share trading happens on the secondary market.

## Influence of Government on Financial Management

### Monetary Policy (RBA)
- The **Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA)** uses monetary policy to assist the federal government achieve **low inflation and economic growth**
- The RBA buys and sells securities/loans to put pressure on **interest rates** and alter the economic cycle
- **Impact on businesses:** If interest rates increase → debt servicing costs increase → higher loan repayment costs

### ASIC (Australian Securities and Investments Commission)
- Enforces and administers the **Corporations Act**
- Protects consumers in investments, insurance, superannuation and banking
- Aims to **reduce fraud and unfair practices** in financial markets
- Ensures companies adhere to the law and makes company information available to the public

### Company Taxation
- Companies pay **company tax on profits** before dividends are distributed to shareholders
- Australia's company tax rate: **30%** for large companies, **25%** for base rate entities (turnover < $50M)
- Tax impacts financial management by:
  - Reducing net profit available for distribution
  - Making **debt more attractive than equity** (interest payments are tax-deductible)
  - Influencing decisions on dividend payments and retained earnings

## Global Market Influences

Financial risks in global markets are **greater than domestic risks** but necessary for business strategy.

| Influence | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Interest Rates** | Comparison of domestic rates to other countries (e.g. Australia 4.35%, Japan 0.5%, USA 4.25-4.5%) |
| **Availability of Funds** | Ease with which a business can access funds on the international financial market |
| **Global Economic Outlook** | Projected changes to economic growth throughout the world |

**These are uncontrollable external factors** — a single firm cannot change global interest rates or economic trends; it can only develop internal strategies to mitigate their impact.

**Key risks for Australian businesses:**
- **Borrowing overseas:** If AUD depreciates, foreign loan becomes more expensive to repay
- **Negative global outlook:** Lenders become cautious → harder to find loans, higher interest rates
- **Factors affecting access to global funds:** Business reputation, cash flowing through international banks, government rules, currency strength

## Sources of Finance

### Internal Sources of Finance
Recorded under **equity** in the balance sheet. From the business owners or business activities.

| Source | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Owner's equity** | Funds contributed by owners or partners to establish and build the business |
| **Retained profit** | Net profit reinvested in the business (added to equity, increases owner's claim) |
| **Sale of assets** | Selling non-current assets the business no longer needs |

### External Sources — Short-Term Debt (≤ 12 months)

| Source | Description | Key features |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| **Overdraft** | Flexibility to borrow from bank at short notice through cheque/current account | Most flexible debt source; interest only on amount used; tax-deductible; better flexibility than loans |
| **Commercial bill** | Written loan order guaranteed by the business's bank | 30-180 days; usually over $100,000 |
| **Factoring** | Selling accounts receivable to a specialist factoring firm | Up to 90% of receivables within 48 hours; riskiest due to unpaid debts (risk borne by agency) |

### External Sources — Long-Term Debt (> 12 months)

| Source | Description | Key features |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| **Mortgage** | Loan repaid over set years with interest, used to purchase land/property | Property becomes security; bank takes property if business defaults |
| **Debenture** | Long-term debt offered via prospectus on securities exchange | Set interest rate for fixed time; more risky than mortgage (no property security); interest not affected by profit level |
| **Unsecured notes (bonds)** | Notes issued by finance companies to gain funds | Higher interest rates reflecting greater risk; no security |
| **Leasing** | Leasing non-current assets (cars, equipment) for payments | Tax-deductible lease costs; reduces need for large upfront payment |

**Types of leases:**
- **Operating lease** — short-term, shorter than asset life, owner maintains asset
- **Financial lease** — leasor purchases asset on behalf of lessee, for the lifetime of the asset

**Why lease instead of buy?** Tax deductible, flexibility (short-term need), spreads cost over time (better cash flow)

### External Sources — Equity Finance

| Source | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Private equity** | Current owners invite people/organisations to invest (Pty Ltd companies); shareholders don't necessarily have control |
| **Public equity** | Companies listed on ASX; anyone can invest |

**Types of public equity:**
1. **Ordinary shares** — issued on ASX, may or may not have voting rights
2. **New share issues** — shares sold for the first time (IPO or new offering)
3. **Rights issue** — existing shareholders offered the right to buy more shares
4. **Placements** — additional shares offered to specific institutions (venture capitalists)
5. **Share purchase plans** — existing shareholders buy more shares without brokerage fees, at below-normal prices (requires ASIC permission)

**Advantages of equity:** No debt servicing costs; no dividend obligation during losses
**Disadvantages:** Dilutes ownership; no guarantee of dividends

### Financing Grants
- Financial gifts from government to assist businesses to establish
- Must meet strict criteria: location, personal characteristics, industry type, goods/services provided
- Criteria include: Indigenous people, regional areas, IT industry, innovative products, essential services
- Government doesn't get repaid or receive ownership — return is through employment, growth and taxes generated

## Financial Reports

### 1. Income Statement (Profit & Loss)

Shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period.

```
Revenue (Sales)
- Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit
- Operating Expenses
= Net Profit (before tax)
- Tax
= Net Profit (after tax)
```

**Key terms:**
- **Gross Profit** = Revenue - COGS (profit from core business activity)
- **Net Profit** = Gross Profit - All Expenses (bottom line)
- **EBIT** = Earnings Before Interest and Tax (more precise measure of operational profitability)

### 2. Balance Sheet

Shows the financial position at a point in time.

```
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
```

**Current Assets** (convertible to cash within 12 months): cash, accounts receivable, inventory, marketable securities

**Non-Current Assets** (long-term): property, plant, equipment, investments

**Current Liabilities** (due within 12 months): accounts payable, short-term debt, overdraft

**Non-Current Liabilities** (due after 12 months): long-term loans, bonds

**Owner's Equity** = contributed capital + retained earnings

### 3. Cash Flow Statement

Shows actual cash inflows and outflows from:
- **Operating activities** (day-to-day business)
- **Investing activities** (buying/selling assets)
- **Financing activities** (loans, dividends, share issues)

**Formula:** Opening Cash Balance + Cash Receipts - Cash Payments = Closing Cash Balance

**Why it matters:** A business can show good profit on the Income Statement but still have cash flow problems because:
1. High credit sales (accounts receivable) — recorded as revenue before cash received
2. Holding unsaleable stock — ties up cash
3. Negative cash flow = can't pay short-term debt

## Financial Ratios

### Profitability Ratios

Measure earning performance and capacity to maximise profits.

| Ratio | Formula | What it shows |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| **Gross Profit Margin** | (Gross Profit / Revenue) × 100 | Revenue left after COGS |
| **Net Profit Margin** | (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100 | Revenue left after ALL expenses |
| **Return on Equity (ROE)** | (Net Profit after tax / Owner's Equity) × 100 | Return earned for owners' investment |
| **Return on Assets (ROA)** | (Net Profit / Total Assets) × 100 | How effectively assets generate profit |

**Comparison rules:** No "ideal" ratio — compare to:
- Previous periods (trend analysis)
- Industry averages (benchmarking)

**Example:**
- Gross Profit $800,000, Net Profit $150,000, Owner's Equity $500,000, Revenue $1,000,000
- GPM = 800,000/1,000,000 × 100 = **80%**
- NPM = 150,000/1,000,000 × 100 = **15%**
- ROE = 150,000/500,000 × 100 = **30%**

### Liquidity Ratios

Measure ability to pay short-term debts.

| Ratio | Formula | Ideal | What it shows |
|-------|---------|-------|---------------|
| **Current Ratio** | Current Assets / Current Liabilities | **2:1** | Short-term debt coverage |
| **Quick Ratio (Acid Test)** | (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities | **1:1** | Stricter test — excludes slow-selling inventory |

**Ideal = 2:1** because there's no guarantee all inventory sells or all receivables are collected. The buffer ensures debts can be paid.

**Example:** CA $200,000, CL $100,000 → CR = 200,000/100,000 = **2:1** ✓

### Gearing (Solvency) Ratios

Measure long-term financial stability and debt reliance.

| Ratio | Formula | Ideal | What it shows |
|-------|---------|-------|---------------|
| **Debt-to-Equity** | Total Liabilities / Owner's Equity | **1:1 (100%)** | How much debt vs equity financing |
| **Gearing Ratio** | (Non-Current Liabilities / Total Assets) × 100 | Lower is better | Proportion of long-term debt funding assets |

**Total Liabilities = Current Liabilities + Non-Current Liabilities**

- Above 1:1 = too much reliance on debt (solvency risk)
- Below 0.5:1 = not taking advantage of debt financing (leverage)

**Example:** TL $300,000, OE $500,000 → D/E = 300,000/500,000 = **0.6:1 or 60%** ✓

### Efficiency Ratios

Measure how effectively resources are used.

| Ratio | Formula | Ideal | What it shows |
|-------|---------|-------|---------------|
| **Expense Ratio** | Total Expenses / Revenue | Lower is better | How much of revenue goes to expenses |
| **Accounts Receivable Turnover** | 365 / (Revenue / Accounts Receivable) | **30 days** | How quickly customers pay |
| **Inventory Turnover** | COGS / Average Inventory | Higher is better | How quickly stock sells |
| **Creditor Turnover** | (Accounts Payable / Purchases) × 365 | Higher = better cash flow | How long to pay suppliers |

**AR Turnover Steps:**
1. Times = Revenue / Accounts Receivable (how many times per year)
2. Days = 365 / Times

**Example:** Revenue $1,000,000, AR $35,000
- Times = 1,000,000 / 35,000 = 28.57
- Days = 365 / 28.57 = **13 days** ✓ (well under 30)

**If AR turnover > 30 days:** poor credit policies, impacts cash flow → use factoring or discounts for early payment

## Financial Management Strategies

### Cash Flow Management Strategies

| Strategy | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|----------|-------------|------|------|
| **Distribution of payments** | Spread expenses over the year (e.g. monthly insurance) | Equal cash outflows | May cost more overall |
| **Prepay expenses** | Pay rent/interest in advance during high-revenue periods | Reduces future outflows | Uses cash now |
| **Discounts for early payment** | Offer discount to customers who pay early (e.g. 2/10 net 30) | Faster cash inflow | Reduces total revenue |
| **Factoring** | Sell accounts receivable to a debt collection agency | Instant cash injection (24-48 hrs) | Fee charged (e.g. 10%), reduces total receivables |

**Factoring example:** Business sells $150,000 AR at 10% fee → receives $135,000, agency gets $15,000.

### Working Capital Management

**Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities**

Must be positive and managed effectively for day-to-day operations.

**Control over Current Assets:**
1. **Cash** — most liquid asset. Increase by encouraging cash sales, quick AR collection
2. **Accounts Receivable** — monitor turnover, enforce credit policies (target: 30 days)
3. **Inventory** — physical inspections, security (magnetic tags, guards), limit staff access

**Control over Current Liabilities:**
1. **Trade creditors** — ensure funds available to pay within terms (usually 30 days)
2. **Short-term loans** — depends on amount and credit rating
3. **Bank overdraft** — promptly deposit cash received, use online monitoring systems

**Working Capital Strategies:**
- **Leasing** — lease equipment instead of buying (avoids large cash outlay)
- **Sale and leaseback** — sell non-current asset for cash injection, then lease it back

### Profitability Strategies

Split into **cost control** and **revenue control**.

#### Cost Control

**Fixed costs** don't change with output (rent, salaries, leases)
**Variable costs** change with output (wages, overtime, advertising, raw materials)

**Strategies to reduce variable costs:**
- Negotiate discounts with suppliers (requires long-term relationships)
- Supplier rationalisation — reduce number of suppliers for bulk buying power
- Switch to cheaper suppliers (must maintain quality)
- Replace labour with technology/automation
- Outsource non-core functions (call centres, payroll, cleaning)
- Increase worker productivity through training or machinery

**Cost Centres:**
- Set up by function, region, or product
- Each centre has a budget and manager responsible for costs
- Monitor by comparing budget vs actual, make adjustments
- Regional centres avoid foreign tax/exchange rate issues

#### Revenue Control

- **Sales forecasting** via sales budgets — predict sales, monitor marketing performance
- **Marketing mix analysis** — identify best promotional strategy and best-selling products
- **Break-even analysis (CVP)** — determine sales volume needed for target profit
- **Optimal sales mix** — focus on highest-margin products

### Break-Even Analysis

**Break-Even Point (units) = Fixed Costs / (Selling Price per unit - Variable Cost per unit)**

**Break-Even Point ($) = BEP units × Selling Price**

At break-even: Total Revenue = Total Costs (no profit, no loss)

**Margin of Safety** = Actual sales - Break-even sales

**Target Profit (units)** = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) / Contribution per unit

## Global Financial Strategies

When operating globally, additional risks must be considered.

### Exchange Rates
- Exchange rates are the value of one country's currency calculated using another country's currency
- **Appreciation** = AUD buys more foreign currency (exports become more expensive for foreign buyers, imports cheaper for Australians)
- **Depreciation** = AUD buys less foreign currency (exports become cheaper for foreign buyers, imports more expensive)

**Impact examples:**
- Importing from China + AUD depreciates → costs more for Australian business
- Borrowing from US + AUD appreciates → less to repay
- Lending to UK + AUD depreciates → receive less money back
- Exporting to France + AUD appreciates → costs Australian business more

### Methods of International Payments

| Method | How it works | Risk |
|--------|-------------|------|
| **Payment in advance** | Exporter receives payment before sending goods | Risk for importer (goods may never be sent) |
| **Letter of credit** | Bank document authorising seller to draw specific amount from buyer's bank under certain conditions | Bank ensures exporter gets paid; some risk for importer |
| **Bills of exchange** | Written order from seller requesting importer pay specified amount at specified time | Bank ensures importer receives goods and exporter is paid |
| **Clean payment** | Goods shipped before payment received | Significant risk for exporter; only used with trusted partners |

### Hedging

**Hedging** = any strategy used by a business to reduce financial risks. Like buying insurance — protection against unforeseen risks. Businesses hedge when they are **uncertain of the future**. Done to reduce foreign exchange and interest rate risks.

#### Natural Hedging
- Setting up **subsidiaries in other countries** to pay for expenses/goods purchased from that country
- Setting up **bank accounts** in countries where the business buys/sells goods, using those funds for local expenses

#### Derivatives (Types of Hedging Contracts)

**1. Forward Exchange Contract**
- Business enters agreement with FOREX business guaranteeing a certain exchange rate on a certain day
- **Advantage:** If spot rate is less favourable (AUD depreciated), the contract rate is better
- **Disadvantage:** If spot rate is more favourable (AUD appreciated), the contract rate is worse

**2. Currency Options Contract**
- Business has the **option** (not obligation) to buy or sell foreign currency when exchange rate movement is advantageous
- Business can choose to use the spot rate instead if it's better
- Protected against unfavourable fluctuations but can still benefit from favourable movements

**3. Swap Contract**
- Written contract allowing Australian business to pay overseas supplier in another currency at a spot rate
- **Example:** Australian business has excess USD, needs to pay Chinese company → Chinese company accepts USD to pay their American suppliers

## Limitations on Financial Statements

Financial reports can be complicated and may not give a clear picture of financial performance and position.

### Normalised Earnings
- Earnings adjusted for cyclical ups and downs of economies
- On the P&L and Balance Sheet, earnings are adjusted to remove **unusual or one-off influences** (e.g. sale of land making a capital gain)
- Normalised earnings show a **true reflection of earnings from operations**
- **Risk of misuse:** Can be used to mislead stakeholders — revenues could be delayed/accelerated, costs manipulated

### Capitalised Expenses
- Purchase price of non-current assets is added to setup costs and used as the asset value in the Balance Sheet
- This value is then **written off over time as depreciation** in the Income Statement
- **Example:** Equipment purchased for $1,000,000 + delivery $500 + installation $1,500 + training $5,000 = $1,007,000 asset value
- **Risk of misuse:** Inappropriately capitalising expenses to inflate profits is illegal

### Valuing Assets
- Balance Sheet normally follows **historical cost concept** (record at purchase price)
- However, asset values change over time:
  - **Appreciation** (land, property) — need at least 2 independent valuations to revalue upward
  - **Depreciation** (equipment, wear and tear) — value decreases over time

**Depreciation formula (straight-line):**
```
Depreciation = (Historical Cost - Disposal Value) / Useful Lifetime
```
**Example:** $1,000,000 equipment, 10-year life, $1,000 disposal value
- Depreciation = (1,000,000 - 1,000) / 10 = $99,900/year
- Year 1 Balance Sheet: $1,000,000 - $99,900 = $900,100
- Year 2 Balance Sheet: $1,000,000 - $199,800 = $800,200

### Timing Issues
- Financial reports cover activities over a period (usually one year)
- **Matching principle:** Revenue must be matched to all expenses incurred in earning that revenue in that accounting period (even if cash not yet paid/received)
- Seasonal fluctuations may distort the true position
- Recording transactions outside the current year means they won't appear on current reports

### Debt Repayments
- Reports cannot disclose specific information about some debt repayments
- **Example:** Money owed for annual leave/sick leave — difficult to predict when workers will take leave

### Notes to Financial Statements
- Additional details left out of main reporting documents (balance sheet, income statements)
- Normally appear in appendices of Annual Reports and/or Prospectus
- Not regulated as strictly as the main report — can potentially be used to mislead

## Ethical Issues in Financial Management

### Accounting Standards
Accountants must display: **integrity, objectivity, confidentiality, high professional/technical ability**

Financial managers and accountants **cannot be 'creative'** when recording transactions to make the business appear more profitable.

**Standards:**
- **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards)
- **AAS** (Australian Accounting Standards) — adapted from IFRS for the Australian market with specific requirements
- Business accounts must be **audited by independent organisations** (including ASIC) to ensure accounts present a true and fair representation

### Unethical Financial Practices

**1. Window Dressing**
- Using **legal techniques** to show a better financial position than reality
- Example: Selling bulk stock to valued customers at end of year at discounted prices → improved cash flow and low stock on Balance Sheet, but doesn't show true liquidity

**2. Income Smoothing (Timing Issues)**
- Reducing taxation by reducing Net Profit
- Example: Recording pre-paid insurance ($10,000 for the year) entirely in the current financial year instead of splitting $5,000/$5,000 → reduces profit → less tax
- Ethically may not show true financial performance

**3. Continuous Disclosure**
- Legally, businesses don't have to inform shareholders of likely losses after previously indicating profits
- **Ethically**, businesses should provide this information so shareholders can make informed decisions
- Limited disclosure requirements during takeovers

**4. Valuing Intangible Assets**
- Difficult to value (goodwill, trademarks, patents)
- Example: Facebook bought WhatsApp for $19 billion — WhatsApp had never made a profit but had 45 million users. Should it be valued at purchase price or potential future value?

**5. Valuing Assets**
- Legally needs independent valuation, but businesses can get multiple valuers and **choose the one that suits their desired position**
- Legal but ethically may not show true and fair position

**6. Debt Repayments**
- Legally must make provision for annual leave and long service leave
- Annual leave is easier to calculate (most employees will use it within a year)
- Long service leave (after 10 years) requires assessing probability of employee staying → ethically challenging to value this future liability accurately

**Other ethical issues:** Inappropriate use of funds, fictitious revenues, hidden liabilities and expenses, improper disclosures/omissions, fraudulent asset valuations, corporate raiding

## Budgets

**Purpose:** Plan for achieving set outcomes, establish standards for monitoring

**Types of budgets:**
- **Operating budgets** — main activities (sales, production, raw materials, labour)
- **Project budgets** — capital expenditure and R&D
- **Financial budgets** — forecast funds required for inputs and anticipated inflows from future sales

### Variance Analysis

| Variance Type | Favourable (F) | Adverse (A) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue variance | Actual > Budget | Actual < Budget |
| Cost variance | Actual costs < Budget | Actual costs > Budget |
| Profit variance | Actual profit > Budget | Actual profit < Budget |

**Variance = Actual − Budget**

## Record Systems & Auditing

- Systems to ensure data recording is accurate, reliable, efficient and accessible
- Minimising errors is important for legal requirements (shareholders, ATO)
- Most errors in computerised systems occur from **human error** during data entry
- All public companies legally require an **annual audit** by qualified independent external auditors
- Auditors check financial records to ensure accounts show a **true and fair view**

## Financial Risks

Risks that a business cannot pay debts as they fall due:
1. Theft of goods
2. Not meeting sales targets
3. Non-payment by accounts receivable
4. Interest rate rises

**Relationship:** Higher borrowings = higher risk (more fixed interest costs, less flexibility, higher insolvency risk)

## HSC Response Framework for Finance Questions

### Ratio Comparison Structure
1. **State the ratio** — "The current ratio has worsened from 1.43:1 in 2022 to 1.16:1 in 2023"
2. **Define what it measures** — "The current ratio measures liquidity, which is the ability to pay current liabilities with current assets"
3. **Identify similarity** — "One similarity is that inventory remained around $52 million"
4. **Identify difference** — "One difference is the increase in short-term borrowing of $2 million"
5. **Link to cause** — "Therefore this shows why the current ratio has worsened"

### Financial Strategy Recommendation Structure
1. **Recommend** — "It is recommended that [business] use [strategy]"
2. **Define** — "[Strategy] is..."
3. **Reason 1** — How it improves performance
4. **Reason 2** — Additional benefit
5. **Link** — Restate recommendation with justification

### Common Finance Question Verbs

| Verb | What to Do | Marks |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **Identify** | Name/state a concept | 1-2 |
| **Describe** | Give features/characteristics | 2-3 |
| **Explain** | How/why with example | 3-4 |
| **Analyse** | Break down + examine relationships | 4-5 |
| **Discuss** | Present multiple perspectives | 5-6 |
| **Evaluate** | Make judgement with supporting arguments | 7-8 |
