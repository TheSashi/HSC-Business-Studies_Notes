# Crichton Office Supplies Report and Apple Marketing Essay

## Question 25: Crichton Office Supplies Business Report

Prompt: Crichton Office Supplies operates out of a warehouse in Western Sydney. They import a range of products and sell them to small businesses, who are mostly business owners ordering office supplies. In order to control costs, the business only has limited amounts of stock on hand and pays their workers the minimum wage under their award contract. While this has led to lower costs, it has had several negative impacts on the business, including delayed customer orders because products were not in stock, low worker satisfaction, and increasing staff turnover that requires recruiting and training new staff. Management is considering changing inventory management, improving worker conditions by moving from an award to an enterprise agreement, and finding other ways to maintain staff. The question is worth 20 marks. You have been hired as a consultant by Crichton Office Supplies to write a business report to the owners. In your report, discuss holding stock in greater quantities for this business, compare the use of an award to an enterprise agreement for Crichton Office Supplies, and recommend human resource strategies to improve the maintenance of staff.

### CRICHTON OFFICE SUPPLIES BUSINESS REPORT

#### EXECUTIVE SUMMARY


Crichton Office Supplies imports office products for small business customers from its Western Sydney warehouse. Limited stock delays orders, while minimum award wages coincide with low satisfaction and rising staff turnover. This report assesses the benefits and costs of holding greater quantities, compares the award with an enterprise agreement, and recommends one human resource strategy, a balanced rewards system. Performance management will support staff consultation and review of the rewards. These actions aim to improve customer availability and retention while keeping costs sustainable.

#### 1. HOLDING STOCK IN GREATER QUANTITIES


Crichton should hold greater quantities selectively using just in case inventory management, which keeps stock available in anticipation of demand. The Business Studies notes identify the relevant benefits: stock can be available to meet customer demand, customer lead times are reduced, bulk buying can lower costs, and alternatives may be available if one item is out of stock. For Crichton, holding more of frequently ordered stationery, paper and toner would allow small business customers’ orders to be dispatched sooner instead of waiting for imported products. Availability improves speed and dependability, supports customer satisfaction and repeat orders, and avoids losing an immediate sale when a product is unavailable. Larger orders may also secure bulk buying savings and reduce the unit purchase cost. However, excess inventory has holding costs, including warehouse space, insurance, security and handling. It ties up working capital that could be used elsewhere and risks theft, damage or obsolescence. A blanket increase could crowd the Western Sydney warehouse and divert cash from wages or other operations. Crichton should therefore use sales records and supplier lead times to set reorder points and safety stock for fast moving products, while keeping lower quantities of slow movers. Management should monitor stockouts, customer lead times, inventory turnover and carrying costs. This targeted increase delivers the stated benefits without stockpiling every product.

#### 2. AWARD COMPARED WITH ENTERPRISE AGREEMENT


An award is an industry or occupation specific set of minimum wages and conditions established by the Fair Work Commission. It is a safety net that applies by default, giving Crichton a clear legal baseline and relatively simple administration. Keeping pay at the award minimum may help control immediate wage costs, but an award is not tailored to this warehouse or its employees. In this scenario, low worker satisfaction and increasing turnover show that meeting the minimum has not been enough to maintain staff. An enterprise agreement is negotiated collectively at a specific workplace between the employer and employees, often with union involvement. Unlike the standardised award, it can tailor pay and conditions to Crichton, such as higher wages, predictable rosters or training arrangements. It must pass the Better Off Overall Test, meaning employees must be better off overall than under the relevant award, and it cannot remove National Employment Standards. Negotiating with employees gives them a voice in conditions and can improve acceptance, satisfaction and commitment. Better conditions may reduce resignations and therefore lower repeated recruitment, induction and training costs. The trade off is that higher pay and bargaining require money and management time. Overall, the award is simpler and may cost less immediately, while an affordable, compliant enterprise agreement is more responsive to Crichton’s retention problem and may reduce longer term replacement costs.

#### 3. RECOMMENDATION: REWARDS


Crichton should introduce one balanced rewards strategy because the maintenance stage of the HR cycle aims to keep existing employees motivated, loyal and retained. The reward package should combine fair remuneration with recognition of team performance, rather than relying on minimum pay alone. A team bonus could recognise accurate order picking, dependable dispatch and positive customer feedback, since warehouse employees rely on one another to complete orders. Crichton should also offer non monetary rewards such as public recognition, greater job variety or flexible working hours, as employees value different benefits. Performance management should support this single rewards strategy: managers and employees can agree clear, fair criteria, discuss progress regularly and use two way feedback to learn which rewards employees value. This prevents speed targets from encouraging rushed errors and allows managers to recognise good work or address concerns early. The business can review satisfaction, turnover, absenteeism, order errors and customer delays to assess whether the reward system is working. A relevant and affordable package can improve satisfaction and loyalty, retain experienced staff and reduce recruitment and training costs. It also links employee effort to the service reliability Crichton’s customers need.

#### CONCLUSION


Crichton should increase stock selectively to improve availability, meet demand, reduce lead times and capture bulk buying savings, while controlling holding costs. An enterprise agreement is more adaptable than relying only on an award if it is affordable and passes the required test. The single recommended HR strategy is a balanced rewards system, supported by performance management to make it fair and assess its effect on retention.

## Question 27: Essay

Prompt: Assess how influences on marketing impact the marketing process. Question 27 is selected.

### RESPONSE

Marketing influences have a substantial impact on the marketing process because they shape what a business researches, which customers it targets, the objectives it sets and the strategies it implements. The process moves through situational analysis, market research, establishing objectives, identifying target markets, developing marketing strategies, and implementation, monitoring and control. Apple demonstrates that consumer choice, economic conditions, competition, government regulation and ethical expectations can redirect these stages. Their effects are not limited to one advertisement or product, because evidence at each stage informs the next. These influences create opportunities when Apple adapts, but can also cause lost sales, penalties and damage to trust when decisions miss customer expectations or legal requirements.

Consumer choice has a significant impact on Apple’s market research and identification of target markets because customer preferences determine what the business needs to know before committing to products and promotion. Psychological factors such as perception and quality expectations, as well as sociocultural trends such as social media, gaming and streaming, shape what customers value in a phone. Apple uses post purchase feedback surveys, including monthly country by country surveys, to identify purchase drivers, popular features, demographics and customer satisfaction, giving it current primary information rather than depending only on broad reports. This research can influence product development and targeting. For example, Apple released larger iPhones in 2014 in response to customer demand, while its marketing promotes camera, screen and processing features valued by customers using social media and streaming. Marketing then carries this evidence forward, allowing Apple to refine objectives and adapt its offer for the customers it has identified. However, the iPhone 5c shows that consumer choice can be misread, as colour options did not outweigh stronger quality expectations. Therefore, research into changing preferences has a substantial effect, but Apple must continually test assumptions rather than rely on one feature or an established image.

Economic and competitive influences have a substantial impact on Apple’s situational analysis, objectives and marketing strategies because they alter customers’ ability to spend and the alternatives available to them. A recession can reduce disposable income and confidence, making customers more price sensitive, while strong competition forces Apple to explain why its premium products are worth their price. A SWOT analysis can identify Apple’s brand reputation as a strength, its high prices as a weakness, and lower priced Asian competitors such as Xiaomi and OPPO as threats. These findings guide objectives such as defending market share without abandoning premium positioning, while helping Apple choose which customer segments to prioritize and how to balance premium devices with more accessible options. The vault’s case study records declining iPhone sales during the Global Financial Crisis, followed by Apple offering the lower priced iPhone 5c and continuing to sell older models. During the COVID 19 period, Apple introduced the iPhone SE and Mini versions for price sensitive buyers. These decisions can widen Apple’s target market and support sales, but too much emphasis on lower prices could weaken premium positioning and margins. Economic and competitive pressures therefore materially shape Apple’s objectives and product and pricing decisions, although each response must be checked against its long term brand strategy.

Government, legal and ethical influences have a decisive impact on Apple’s implementation, monitoring and control because they set boundaries for product claims, consumer rights, privacy and market access. Apple’s iPhone 6 launch in China was delayed amid privacy concerns, demonstrating that a marketing plan cannot simply be applied identically in every country. Regulation also affected Apple’s expansion in India, while government incentives for local smartphone manufacturing created an opportunity to adjust market entry and production plans. In Australia, Apple received a 9 million dollar penalty over misrepresentations about consumer rights and gave an undertaking to improve its consumer guarantee policies and practices. This shows that misleading claims about repairs or guarantees can turn marketing into legal and reputational risk, forcing changes to promotion and after sales communication. Apple must therefore monitor customer feedback, verify claims and adapt distribution and promotion to local rules. Its monitoring should compare complaints and sales with objectives so that corrective action follows changes in customer or legal expectations. These influences constrain Apple’s choices and add compliance costs, but ignoring them is more damaging because penalties and lost trust undermine loyalty and sales.

Overall, influences affect every stage of Apple’s marketing process, but consumer choice and economic and competitive conditions have the greatest ongoing effect because they determine what customers value and whether Apple’s offer remains attractive. Government, legal and ethical requirements may not create customer preferences, yet they can decisively limit launches and promotional claims. Apple’s surveys, product adjustments and policy changes show that the process works best as a continuous cycle of research, action and control. Apple can protect its market position only when it adapts to customers and competitors while maintaining truthful, lawful marketing, which preserves trust and supports repeat purchases.
