## 1. Role of Operations

**Definition**: Operations is the business process of **transformation** and **value adding** — converting inputs into outputs (goods and/or services) that have greater worth than the original inputs.

### 1.1 Transformation

- **Transformation** = the process of converting inputs into outputs
- Raw materials, labour, capital and information are combined through physical, chemical, or service-related changes
- Manufacturing = physical transformation (steel → car; fabric → clothing)
- Services = knowledge/skills transformation (untrained student → skilled graduate; problem → legal advice)

### 1.2 Value Adding

- **Value adding** = creating extra value as inputs become outputs
- The difference between the cost of inputs and the price charged for outputs is the value added
- Example: A restaurant buys raw ingredients for $5 but sells a prepared meal for $25 → $20 of value added
- Value added = profit potential + contribution to fixed costs + employee wages

> **Apple**: Apple buys components (screens, chips, casings) for relatively low cost, transforms them at Foxconn factories in China, and sells finished iPhones at $1,000+. The value added is enormous — reflecting design, brand, software, and user experience.

### 1.3 Operations and Customer Focus

Operations must align with what customers demand:

| Customer Want | Operations Response |
|---|---|
| Waste minimal resources | Lean production, elimination of waste (muda) |
| Reflect fair value for labour | Efficient workforce management, appropriate wages |
| Operate at low cost | Cost reduction through technology, economies of scale |
| Integrate environmental awareness | Sustainable production, recycling, reduced emissions |
| Reflect changing customer needs | Flexible production, responsive supply chains |

> **BYD**: Customers want affordable, reliable EVs. BYD's operations focus on mass-producing the "Blade Battery" at low cost while maintaining quality — adding value through innovation rather than premium pricing.

### 1.4 Strategic Role of Operations

- Operations contributes to the **strategic direction** of the organisation
- Strategic decisions are long-term and affect the entire business
- Overarching goal: **maximise profit** through increasing revenue or minimising expenses

### 1.5 Cost Leadership

**Definition**: A strategy of achieving the **lowest costs** in the industry, allowing the business to be the most price competitive while remaining profitable.

**Economies of Scale**: Cost advantages that arise from increased operations/production:

| Type | How It Reduces Costs | Apple Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Purchasing economies** | Bulk buying reduces per-unit costs | Apple buys millions of A-series chips from TSMC at lower unit cost |
| **Technical economies** | Better use of technology and plant | Automated assembly lines at Foxconn produce iPhones faster with fewer defects |
| **Spreading fixed costs** | Same factory rent/insurance spread over more units | Producing 200M+ iPhones/year spreads R&D costs across every device |
| **Marketing economies** | Global branding and advertising shared across products | One Apple logo marketed once covers iPhone, iPad, Mac, Watch |
| **Training economies** | One training program serves many workers | Foxconn trains workers on standardised procedures across shifts |

> **McDonald's**: Over 42,000 restaurants worldwide means ingredients are purchased in massive bulk — lettuce, potatoes, beef all at the lowest possible price. Standardised kitchen equipment and processes reduce training costs. Fixed costs (head office, global branding) are spread across millions of transactions daily.

### 1.6 Product Differentiation

**Definition**: Distinguishing goods and services from competitors to create a **unique marketspace** → increased sales and market coverage.

**Goods differentiation**:
- Vary features, quality, design, materials
- Augmented features: warranties, delivery options, after-sales service
- Example: Apple differentiates through design (minimalist), ecosystem (iOS integration), and branding (bitten apple logo)

**Services differentiation**:
- Vary expertise, qualifications, response time
- Example: McDonald's differentiates through speed of service, consistent quality, and global availability

### 1.7 Goods vs Services

| Feature | Goods | Services |
|---|---|---|
| **Tangibility** | Tangible — can be seen, touched, stored | Intangible — cannot be stored; produced and consumed simultaneously |
| **Customisation** | Standardised (Ford Model T) or customised (bespoke suit) | Standardised (McDonald's drive-thru) or customised (legal advice) |
| **Quality measurement** | Measured by durability, fitness for purpose | Measured by professionalism, reliability, responsiveness |
| **Inventory** | Can be held in stock as assets | Cannot be stored — perishable (empty airline seat = lost revenue) |
| **Transportation** | Can be transported and distributed globally | Produced and consumed at same time and place (haircut, dental) |
| **Customer involvement** | Low — produced away from customer | High — customer often participates in delivery |
| **Perishability** | Non-perishable (can be stored for years) | Perishable (time-sensitive — lost if not used) |
| **Separability** | Producer can be separated from product | Producer and consumer must be present together |

> **Apple (goods)**: iPhones are tangible, can be stored in warehouses, shipped globally, and inventory managed. **McDonald's (services)**: A Big Mac is produced and consumed within minutes — cannot be stored, stored quality degrades, customer present during production.

## 2. Inputs → Transformation → Outputs

The operations model follows a simple flow: **Inputs → Transformation Process → Outputs**

### 2.1 Transformed Resources (Changed by the Process)

| Resource | Description | Apple Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Materials/Intermediate goods** | Raw materials and components that become part of the final product | Screens (Samsung/LG), chips (TSMC), circuitry, aluminium casings, glass |
| **Information** | Data, market research, customer feedback used to guide operations | Customer surveys, sales data, defect reports, market trends |
| **Customers** | In service businesses, customers themselves are transformed | Not applicable to Apple directly; applies to McDonald's (hungry → fed), universities (untrained → skilled) |

### 2.2 Transforming Resources (Carry Out the Change)

| Resource | Description | Apple Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Human Resources** | Skills, training, motivation, expertise of workers | Foxconn factory workers in China (1M+ workers); Apple engineers in Cupertino; retail "Geniuses" |
| **Facilities** | Plant, machinery, technology, layout, equipment | Foxconn factories, automated assembly lines, robotic arms, testing equipment, packaging lines |

### 2.3 Outputs

| Output | Description |
|---|---|
| **Final goods/services** | The finished product ready for sale (iPhone, Big Mac, haircut) |
| **Customer service** | Support provided with the product (Apple Care, after-sales service) |
| **Warranties** | Promises to repair/replace faulty products (Apple's 1-year warranty) |
| **Waste** | By-products of production — must be managed (packaging waste, defective units, emissions) |

## 3. The 4Vs

The **4Vs** describe the nature of the operations process and help determine what type of operations system is needed.

| V | What It Means | High | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | How much is made | Efficient, automated, low unit cost, standardised | Manual, skilled, high unit cost, personalised |
| **Variety** | Range of different products | Complex, flexible, expensive, many product lines | Simple, efficient, standardised, few products |
| **Variation** | How much demand fluctuates | Need flexible resources, accurate forecasting, overtime capacity | Stable planning, consistent resources, efficient scheduling |
| **Visibility** | Customer contact with process | Skilled staff, responsive, quality interaction, premium pricing | Speed, consistency, cost control, minimal contact |

### 3.1 Trade-offs Between the 4Vs

- **High Volume + High Variety** = expensive and complex (requires flexible manufacturing)
- **High Visibility** = need skilled workers, more training costs, higher labour costs
- **High Variation** = need buffer capacity, overtime, or temporary workers
- **Low Volume + Low Variety** = simple but may miss market opportunities

> **Apple**: High volume (200M+ iPhones/year at Foxconn) + High variety (iPhone, iPad, Mac, Watch, AirPods = many product lines) + High variation (new models cause demand spikes) + High visibility (Apple Stores provide hands-on customer experience). This combination is complex and expensive — but Apple's scale and brand allow it to manage effectively.

> **McDonald's**: High volume (billions of burgers/year) + Low variety (standardised menu) + Low variation (consistent demand patterns) + Low visibility (drive-thru = minimal customer contact with kitchen). This is efficient and low-cost — matching cost leadership strategy.

### 3.2 How the 4Vs Influence Operations Decisions

| 4V | Impact on Operations |
|---|---|
| **High Volume** | Product layout (assembly line), automation, standardised processes, bulk purchasing |
| **High Variety** | Process layout (grouped by function), skilled workers, flexible equipment |
| **High Variation** | Buffer inventory, overtime, flexible contracts, accurate demand forecasting |
| **High Visibility** | Customer-facing staff training, service quality, layout designed for interaction |

## 4. Technology, Task Design & Layout

### 4.1 Technology

**Leading edge** technology = new, untested, innovative (high risk, high reward)
**Established** technology = proven, reliable, industry-standard (low risk, predictable)

| Aspect | Leading Edge | Established |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | High initial investment | Lower, proven costs |
| **Risk** | May fail or become obsolete | Predictable outcomes |
| **Benefit** | Competitive advantage, efficiency gains | Reliability, staff familiarity |
| **Example** | AI quality inspection robots | Standard conveyor belts |

**Benefits of technology in operations**:
- Faster production speed
- Fewer human errors
- Consistent quality
- Labour cost savings long-term
- Data collection for monitoring

**Costs of technology in operations**:
- High initial capital investment
- Staff training required
- Maintenance and repairs
- Technology may become obsolete

> **Apple**: Invests billions in leading-edge technology — robotic disassembly robots (Liam/Daisy) for recycling, automated testing equipment, advanced manufacturing processes. But also relies on established technology (Foxconn assembly lines) for consistent high-volume production.

### 4.2 Task Design

**Definition**: Classifying job activities for easy employee performance.

**Steps in task design**:
1. Define the duties required for each role
2. Analyse duties into specific tasks
3. Allocate difficulty levels and time requirements to each task
4. Match tasks to industry awards (pay rates, conditions)
5. Articulate roles via job descriptors (written job descriptions)

**Impact on performance objectives**:
- Task design affects **speed** (specialised tasks = faster)
- Task design affects **quality** (clear tasks = fewer errors)
- Task design affects **cost** (efficient tasks = lower labour costs)
- Task design affects **flexibility** (cross-trained workers = adaptable)

> **McDonald's**: Task design is extremely specific — the "kitchen production system" breaks down burger-making into individual steps (toast bun, add sauce, add patty, wrap). Each worker is trained on specific tasks → high speed, consistent quality, low training time for new staff.

### 4.3 Layout Types

| Type | Description | When Used | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Process layout** | Resources grouped by function/functionality | High variety, low volume | Hospital (theatre, X-ray, pharmacy), bank, printery |
| **Product layout** | Assembly line; resources arranged in sequence of production | High volume, consistent quality | Car factory, Foxconn iPhone assembly |
| **Fixed position layout** | Product stays in one place; workers and equipment come to it | Very large, immovable products | Construction, ship building, aircraft assembly |

**Process Layout — Advantages**:
- Flexible — can handle a variety of products/tasks
- Equipment shared across tasks → better utilisation
- Workers develop broad skills

**Process Layout — Disadvantages**:
- Less efficient (movement between functional areas)
- Longer production times
- Higher work-in-progress inventory

**Product Layout — Advantages**:
- Highly efficient (minimised movement, specialised stations)
- Fast production speed
- Low unit cost
- Consistent quality

**Product Layout — Disadvantages**:
- Inflexible — difficult to change product
- Boring repetitive work → lower motivation
- One breakdown can stop entire line

> **Apple**: Uses **product layout** at Foxconn — iPhone assembly is a linear process where each worker/station performs one specific task. This enables 200M+ iPhones/year. But Apple also uses **process layout** in its R&D labs where different teams (hardware, software, design) work in functional groups on diverse projects.

> **McDonald's**: Kitchen uses **product layout** — assembly line from order → grill → bun → wrap → bag. Front of house uses **process layout** — counter service, drive-thru, McCafé as separate functional areas.

## 5. Monitoring, Controlling & Improvement

### 5.1 Monitoring

**Definition**: Measuring actual performance against planned performance using **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)**.

**Common Operations KPIs**:

| KPI | What It Measures | Target Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead times** | Time from order to delivery | Under 5 business days |
| **Inventory turnover** | How quickly stock is sold and replaced | 8-12 times per year |
| **Defect rates** | Percentage of faulty products | Less than 0.1% |
| **Capacity utilisation** | How much of available capacity is used | Above 85% |
| **Production costs** | Total cost per unit produced | Decreasing trend |
| **Labour productivity** | Output per worker per hour | Increasing trend |
| **On-time delivery** | Percentage of orders delivered on schedule | Above 95% |
| **Customer complaints** | Number of complaints per period | Decreasing trend |

### 5.2 Controlling

**Definition**: Assessing KPIs against targets, investigating any variations, and taking **corrective action** to ensure objectives are met.

**Controlling process**:
1. Compare actual KPIs against targets
2. Identify variances (positive or negative)
3. Investigate the cause of variance
4. Take corrective action to get back on track

### 5.3 Improvement

**Definition**: Systematic reduction of inefficiencies, waste, and bottlenecks to improve performance.

**Areas of improvement**:
- **Time** — reduce lead times, waiting times, processing times
- **Process flows** — eliminate bottlenecks, streamline workflows
- **Quality** — reduce defects, improve consistency
- **Cost** — reduce production costs per unit
- **Efficiency** — increase output with same or fewer inputs

### 5.4 Continuous Improvement (Kaizen)

**Kaizen** = ongoing commitment to perfection; requires involvement of ALL employees

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Philosophy** | Small, incremental improvements made continuously by everyone |
| **Approach** | Bottom-up — every employee identifies improvement opportunities |
| **Focus** | Eliminate waste (muda), reduce defects, improve efficiency |
| **Timeframe** | Long-term, ongoing — never "finished" |
| **Result** | Gradual but significant cumulative improvement |

> **Apple**: Apple continuously improves its operations — from switching chip suppliers (Samsung → TSMC for better performance), to redesigning manufacturing processes, to implementing robotic recycling (Liam robot disassembles iPhones for material recovery). Each generation of iPhone improves manufacturing efficiency.

> **McDonald's**: Kaizen is embedded — franchisees constantly identify small improvements (faster fryer timers, better kitchen layouts, improved drive-thru workflows). Corporate implements best practices across the system — "One McDonald's Way" means innovations spread globally.

## 6. Performance Objectives

Performance objectives define what the operations function must achieve to support the strategic direction of the business.

| Objective | Definition | Apple Example | McDonald's Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quality** | Design quality + conformance quality | High design quality (sleek, intuitive); conformance issues in past (5M iPhones returned 2013) | Consistent burger quality worldwide; recipe changes (fries 1990) |
| **Speed** | Fast response to market; reduced lead/wait times | Fast product launches (annual iPhone cycle); fast retail service | Drive-thru under 3 minutes; McDelivery in 30 minutes |
| **Dependability** | Consistency and reliability of products/services | iPhones work consistently across years; software updates maintain performance | Every Big Mac tastes the same worldwide |
| **Flexibility** | Ability to adjust quickly (volume, variety, mix) | Can shift production between iPhone models; adjust to demand spikes | Menu customisation (McAloo Tikki India); seasonal items |
| **Customisation** | Individualised products; mass customisation | Engraving; colour options; storage sizes; Apple Watch bands | McCustomise burger (select own toppings); limited-time offers |
| **Cost** | Minimising expenses for maximum competitiveness | iPhone SE = lower-cost components; in-house chips reduce costs | Loose Change Menu (AU$1+); bulk purchasing economies |

### 6.1 Quality of Design vs Quality of Conformance

**Quality of Design**: Arises from understanding consumer preferences and designing products to meet those expectations. Focuses on features, materials, and specifications that customers value.

**Quality of Conformance**: How well the product meets its prescribed specifications. Focuses on manufacturing accuracy and consistency — does every unit meet the standard?

> **Apple quality of design**: Sleek design, intuitive interface, premium materials — reflecting what customers expect from Apple products.
> **Apple quality of conformance**: In 2013, 5 million iPhones were returned due to defects — a conformance failure. $1.6 billion cost. Showed the gap between design intent and manufacturing execution.

### 6.2 Mass Customisation

**Definition**: A standard mass-produced item that is modified to specific customer requirements — combining the efficiency of mass production with the appeal of customisation.

**How it works**:
1. Base product manufactured in high volume (cost efficient)
2. Final modifications made to customer specifications (personalised)
3. Customer gets a unique product at near mass-production prices

> **Apple**: iPhones are mass-produced but offer customisation through colour choices, storage sizes (64GB/128GB/256GB/512GB/1TB), engraving, and carrier-specific models. This is mass customisation — the base is standard but options are personalised.

> **McDonald's**: Burgers are mass-produced (standard patties, buns, sauces) but customers can customise (no pickles, extra sauce, different cheese). The McCustomise campaign in Australia exemplified this approach.

### 6.3 Dependability and Flexibility

**Dependability** means customers can rely on consistent quality and delivery:
- Same product every time
- Delivered on time, every time
- Predictable performance

**Flexibility** means the ability to respond quickly to changes:
- **Volume flexibility** — increase/decrease output quickly
- **Product flexibility** — add new products or modify existing ones
- **Mix flexibility** — produce different combinations of products
- **Delivery flexibility** — change delivery schedules

## 7. Influences on Operations

External and internal factors that affect how a business structures and manages its operations.

### 7.1 Globalisation

**Definition**: The increasing interconnectedness of countries through trade, investment, and technology — removing trade barriers and enabling global production and distribution.

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Removal of trade barriers** | Free trade agreements, reduced tariffs, WTO regulations |
| **Global production facilities** | Operations structured around the most cost-effective locations |
| **Opportunity** | Access to global markets, economies of scale, lower production costs |
| **Threat** | Intensified global competition, domestic job losses, cultural challenges |

> **Apple**: Operations structured globally — components sourced from 30+ countries (Korea, Taiwan, USA, China), assembled in China (Foxconn), sold in 175+ countries. Globalisation enables Apple's scale but exposes it to trade wars (US-China tensions), tariffs, and geopolitical risk.

### 7.2 Technology

| Impact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Increased efficiency** | Automation reduces time and errors |
| **Reduced costs** | Fewer workers needed per unit; lower error costs |
| **Consistent quality** | Machines produce identical outputs every time |
| **Higher productivity** | More output per worker per hour |

**Key technologies in operations**:
- **Automation and robotics** — robotic assembly, automated testing
- **Computer-Aided Design (CAD)** — digital design and simulation before production
- **Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM)** — computer-controlled production processes
- **Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)** — integrated software managing all business processes
- **Artificial Intelligence** — predictive maintenance, quality inspection, demand forecasting

> **Apple**: Uses over 60,000 robots at supplier facilities for iPhone assembly. Robotic arms perform precise tasks (screwing, polishing, testing) with greater consistency than human workers. AI monitors quality in real-time.

### 7.3 Quality Expectations

**Goods quality expectations**:
- Design quality (aesthetic, features)
- Fitness for purpose (does what it's supposed to)
- Durability (lasts a reasonable time)
- Reliability (consistent performance)

**Services quality expectations**:
- Professionalism of staff
- Reliability of service delivery
- Customisation to individual needs

**Consequences of failure**:
- Customer dissatisfaction
- Product returns
- Damaged brand reputation
- Lost sales and market share
- Legal liability (warranties, consumer law)

> **Apple quality failure**: In 2013, Apple had to replace 5 million iPhones due to manufacturing defects — costing $1.6 billion. In 2012, Apple Maps launched with significant errors, damaging Apple's reputation for quality. These failures show what happens when quality expectations are not met.

### 7.4 Cost-Based Competition

- Pressure to lower production costs to remain price competitive
- Methods to reduce costs:
  - Eliminate waste (lean production)
  - Standardise products and processes
  - Produce at high volume (economies of scale)
  - Automate to reduce labour costs
  - Bulk buying of inputs
- **Impact on wages and working conditions**: Cost reduction can lead to lower wages, longer hours, reduced conditions (Foxconn working conditions controversy)

### 7.5 Government Policies and Legal

| Legislation | Impact on Operations |
|---|---|
| **WHS Act** (Workplace Health and Safety) | Safe working conditions, equipment standards, training requirements, penalties for breaches |
| **Fair Work Act** | Wages (minimum wage, penalty rates), working conditions, employee rights, unfair dismissal protections |
| **Environmental Protection Act** | Waste management, emissions controls, recycling requirements, carbon footprint reduction |
| **Competition and Consumer Act** | Fair trading, product safety, warranty requirements, ACCC enforcement |
| **Food Safety Act** | Hygiene standards, handling procedures, temperature controls (McDonald's, restaurants) |

**Compliance costs**: The expenses businesses incur to meet government regulations — new equipment, training, audits, reporting, legal advice.

> **Apple**: Must comply with environmental regulations in every country it operates — recycling programs, hazardous material handling, packaging waste. Apple's "Environment" reports demonstrate compliance but also represent significant operational costs.

### 7.6 Environmental Sustainability

**Definition**: Operating without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

**Methods**:
- Minimise waste in production
- Recycle and reuse materials
- Reduce carbon footprint
- Use sustainable/ethical materials
- Design products for longevity and recyclability

**CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility)**: The **triple bottom line** — measuring success not just by financial profit, but also by social and environmental impact.

**Legal compliance vs Ethical responsibility**:

| | Legal Compliance | Ethical Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | Letter of the law — meeting minimum legal requirements | Spirit of the law — going beyond what's legally required |
| **Motivation** | Avoiding penalties and prosecution | Genuine desire to do the right thing |
| **Example** | Recycling what's legally required | Recycling everything possible, even if not required |
| **Apple** | Meets environmental regulations in each country | Invests in renewable energy, robotic recycling, carbon-neutral goals |

## 8. Supply Chain Management

**Definition**: The integration and management of the flow of supplies from inputs through transformation to outputs — coordinating all activities from sourcing raw materials to delivering finished products to customers.

### 8.1 Sourcing

**Definition**: The process of identifying, evaluating, and selecting suppliers of inputs.

**Factors in sourcing decisions**:

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Consumer demand** | What inputs are needed to meet customer expectations |
| **Quality of inputs** | Must meet quality standards; poor inputs = poor outputs |
| **Flexibility/timeliness** | Suppliers must deliver on time and adjust to demand changes |
| **Cost** | Input costs directly affect production costs and pricing |

### 8.2 Supply Chain Trends

| Trend | Definition | Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supplier rationalisation** | Assessing supplier numbers; reducing to most effective ones | Less contracting, less wastage, stronger relationships | Over-dependence on fewer suppliers |
| **Backwards vertical integration** | Purchasing through mergers/acquisitions of suppliers | Control over supply, reduced costs, guaranteed supply | Capital investment, management complexity |
| **Cost minimisation** | Using offshore suppliers for lower costs | Lower input costs → lower production costs | Quality risk, communication barriers, longer lead times |
| **Flexible/responsive supply chain** | Ordering supplies only when needed; optimal use | Reduced waste, lower inventory costs, responsive to demand | Vulnerable to supply disruptions |

> **Apple supply chain**: Tim Cook (former supply chain manager) transformed Apple's supply chain — reduced suppliers from hundreds to key partners, reduced warehouses from 19 to just a few. This minimised inventory costs and improved efficiency. However, reliance on few suppliers creates vulnerability (2021 chip shortage delayed iPhone 12 production).

### 8.3 E-Commerce in Supply Chain Management

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **E-procurement** | Electronic ordering and purchasing systems | Apple's automated ordering from suppliers via integrated systems |
| **B2B (Business to Business)** | Automatic restocking when stock falls to predetermined levels | Foxconn automatically orders more screens from Samsung when inventory drops |
| **B2C (Business to Consumer)** | Direct sales to consumers via online platforms | Apple.com, McDonald's delivery apps |

### 8.4 Global Sourcing

**Definition**: Purchasing supplies without location constraint — sourcing from the most cost-effective or highest-quality suppliers worldwide.

**Advantages**:
- Lower costs (cheaper labour, materials in developing countries)
- Reduced lead times (sourcing closer to production facilities)
- Access to materials not available domestically
- Diversification of supply risk

**Disadvantages**:
- Quality risk (inconsistent standards across countries)
- Impact on domestic businesses (Australian suppliers lose contracts)
- Communication barriers (language, time zones, cultural differences)
- Geopolitical risks (trade wars, tariffs, political instability)
- Environmental concerns (carbon footprint of global transportation)

> **Apple**: Sources from 30+ countries — chips from Taiwan (TSMC), screens from South Korea (Samsung/LG), glass from USA (Corning), rare earth minerals from Africa/China. Global sourcing gives access to the best components but creates complex logistics and dependency on international relationships.

### 8.5 Logistics

**Definition**: The management of the flow of goods from origin to consumption — including distribution, transportation, storage, materials handling, and packaging.

| Activity | Description |
|---|---|
| **Distribution** | Getting products from production to the customer |
| **Transportation** | Physical movement of goods (road, rail, sea, air) |
| **Storage/Warehousing** | Holding inventory until needed |
| **Distribution centres** | Hubs where goods are received, sorted, and dispatched |
| **Materials handling** | Moving goods within facilities (forklifts, conveyors) |
| **Packaging** | Protecting goods during transport; also for branding and marketing |

## 9. Inventory Management

### 9.1 Holding Stock (Just in Case — JIC)

**Definition**: Maintaining buffer inventory to meet unexpected demand or supply disruptions.

**Advantages**:

| # | Advantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | Meet consumer demand when stock is available |
| 2 | Provide alternative products if a product line runs out |
| 3 | Reduce lead times for customers |
| 4 | Generate immediate revenue from available stock |
| 5 | Distribute to distribution centres for wider availability |
| 6 | Promote in non-traditional markets (stock can be moved where needed) |
| 7 | Sell older stock at reduced prices (clearance sales) |
| 8 | Stock is an asset shown on the Balance Sheet (improves financial position) |
| 9 | Bulk production reduces per-unit costs |

**Disadvantages**:

| # | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Holding costs**: storage fees, spoilage, insurance, theft, handling, staff |
| 2 | **Opportunity cost**: capital, labour, and energy could be invested elsewhere |
| 3 | **Obsolescence risk**: stock becomes outdated or unwanted (technology products) |

### 9.2 Just in Time (JIT)

**Definition**: Producing only what is needed, when it is needed, in the quantity needed — minimising inventory holding.

**Advantages**:

| # | Advantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reduced storage costs (less warehouse space needed) |
| 2 | No theft or damage risk (minimal stock on hand) |
| 3 | No obsolescence (stock is used immediately) |
| 4 | Less capital tied up in inventory (cash available for other uses) |

**Disadvantages**:

| # | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| 1 | Lead time increases with demand variation |
| 2 | Supplier delivery issues can halt production |
| 3 | Production shutdown risk if a single supplier fails |
| 4 | Requires highly reliable suppliers and accurate forecasting |
| 5 | Less buffer if unexpected demand spike occurs |

> **Apple and JIT**: Apple uses a hybrid approach — JIT for most iPhone components (ordered from suppliers to arrive just in time for assembly) but maintains some buffer stock for critical components. During COVID-19, Apple's JIT system was disrupted when suppliers couldn't deliver, delaying iPhone 12 production.

> **McDonald's and JIT**: McDonald's uses JIT principles — ingredients delivered fresh daily to restaurants. Perishable items (lettuce, tomatoes, buns) arrive frequently to maintain freshness. However, stores keep buffer stock of non-perishable items (packaging, frozen patties) — a JIC approach for critical items.

### 9.3 Inventory Valuation Methods

> ⚠️ **IMPORTANT EXAM DISTINCTION**: If the question asks about inventory **MANAGEMENT** → discuss JIC or JIT. If the question asks about inventory **VALUATION** → discuss FIFO or LIFO.

#### FIFO (First In First Out)

**Definition**: The oldest costs (earliest purchased) are assigned to Cost of Goods Sold (COGS); the newest costs remain in inventory on the Balance Sheet.

#### LIFO (Last In First Out)

**Definition**: The newest costs (most recently purchased) are assigned to COGS; the oldest costs remain in inventory on the Balance Sheet.

#### When Each Method Is Preferable

| Goal | Costs Increasing | Costs Decreasing |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimise profit (pay less tax)** | LIFO (higher COGS) | FIFO (higher COGS) |
| **Maximise profit (impress investors)** | FIFO (lower COGS) | LIFO (lower COGS) |

#### Worked Example: FIFO vs LIFO

**Scenario**: A business purchases 3 batches of inventory:

| Batch | Units | Cost per Unit | Total Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Batch 1 (Jan) | 100 | $10 | $1,000 |
| Batch 2 (Feb) | 100 | $12 | $1,200 |
| Batch 3 (Mar) | 100 | $15 | $1,500 |
| **Total** | **300** | | **$3,700** |

**During the period, 200 units are sold.**

**FIFO Calculation**:
```
COGS:   100 × $10 (Batch 1) + 100 × $12 (Batch 2) = $2,200
Ending Inventory: 100 × $15 (Batch 3) = $1,500
```

**LIFO Calculation**:
```
COGS:   100 × $15 (Batch 3) + 100 × $12 (Batch 2) = $2,700
Ending Inventory: 100 × $10 (Batch 1) = $1,000
```

**Result**: When costs are **increasing**:
- FIFO → lower COGS ($2,200) → higher profit → higher tax
- LIFO → higher COGS ($2,700) → lower profit → lower tax

#### Weighted Average

**Definition**: The average cost of all inventory is used for both COGS and ending inventory — smooths out price fluctuations.

```
Weighted Average Cost = Total Cost of All Inventory / Total Units
= $3,700 / 300 = $12.33 per unit

COGS: 200 × $12.33 = $2,466.67
Ending Inventory: 100 × $12.33 = $1,233.33
```

### 9.4 Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

**Definition**: The optimal order quantity that minimises total inventory costs (ordering costs + holding costs).

```
EOQ = √(2DS / H)

Where:
D = Annual demand (units)
S = Cost per order (setup cost)
H = Holding cost per unit per year
```

**EOQ balances two competing costs**:
- **Ordering costs**: Cost of placing and receiving orders (decreases as order size increases)
- **Holding costs**: Cost of storing inventory (increases as order size increases)

> The EOQ is the point where the total of ordering costs + holding costs is minimised.

## 10. Quality Management

Quality management ensures that products and services consistently meet or exceed customer expectations.

### 10.1 Quality Management Approaches

| Approach | Key Feature | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Quality Control** | Inspection-based | Inspect goods at various production points; define standards, test against standards, take corrective action if defects found |
| **Quality Assurance** | Prevention-based | System to prevent defects from occurring; "right the first time"; ISO 9000 international standards |
| **TQM** (Total Quality Management) | Holistic | Every employee is responsible for quality; manage the total business for quality; customer-focused |
| **Six Sigma** | Data-driven | Target of 3.4 defects per million opportunities; uses DMAIC methodology (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control) |
| **Kaizen** | Continuous improvement | Continuous incremental improvement by all employees; eliminate waste (muda); bottom-up approach |
| **Quality Circles** | Employee groups | Small groups of employees meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems |

### 10.2 DMAIC (Six Sigma)

| Step | Description |
|---|---|
| **Define** | Identify the problem and project goals |
| **Measure** | Collect data on current performance |
| **Analyse** | Identify root causes of defects |
| **Improve** | Develop and implement solutions |
| **Control** | Monitor improvements to sustain gains |

### 10.3 Fitness for Purpose

**Definition**: The product does what it is supposed to do — not necessarily the most expensive or feature-rich, but it fulfils its intended function.

> **Example**: A car that reliably transports a person from A to B is "fit for purpose" — even if it doesn't have sheep leather seats. A $20 watch that tells time accurately is fit for purpose, even though a Rolex costs $10,000.

### 10.4 ISO 9000

**Definition**: International quality management standards that provide a framework for consistent quality processes. ISO 9000 certification demonstrates to customers and stakeholders that a business has quality systems in place.

## 11. Outsourcing

**Definition**: The use of external providers (domestic or global) to perform business activities that were previously done in-house.

### 11.1 Advantages of Outsourcing

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Cost savings** | External providers may have lower labour costs, economies of scale |
| **Efficiency** | Specialists can perform tasks faster and better |
| **Access to specialist skills** | Expertise not available in-house |
| **Focus on core business** | Management can concentrate on main activities |
| **Increased accountability** | Contractual agreements define performance standards |
| **Simplification** | Reduces complexity of operations |

### 11.2 Disadvantages of Outsourcing

| Disadvantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Communication/language barriers** | Especially with global providers |
| **Loss of quality control** | Less direct oversight of production |
| **Security risks** | Intellectual property theft, data breaches |
| **Dependency on partner** | If partner fails, business is disrupted |
| **Loss of corporate memory** | In-house knowledge lost when functions move external |
| **Reputation affected** | Partner's problems become your problems |

> **Apple and Outsourcing**: Apple outsources iPhone production to Foxconn (Taiwan-based manufacturer in China). Benefits: Foxconn has expertise, economies of scale, lower labour costs. Risks: Apple lost control over working conditions (Foxconn worker suicides 2010-2012), quality control issues (2013: 5M defective iPhones), security risks (prototype leaks), and dependency (COVID-19 disruptions). Apple has since diversified to India and Vietnam to reduce risk.

### 11.3 Outsourcing vs In-House Production

| Factor | Outsourcing | In-House |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | Potentially lower (specialist economies of scale) | Higher fixed costs but more control |
| **Quality** | Depends on provider; less direct control | Full control over quality standards |
| **Flexibility** | May be limited by contract terms | Can change quickly as needed |
| **Focus** | Frees management to focus on core business | Requires management attention |
| **Risk** | Dependency, security, reputation risk | Retains all risk but maintains control |

## 13. Resistance to Change

Change in operations is necessary but often meets resistance from employees and stakeholders.

### 13.1 Financial Costs of Change

| Cost | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Purchasing new equipment** | High initial cost; long-term benefits may take years to realise |
| **Redundancy payouts** | Money paid to employees made redundant when roles are eliminated |
| **Retraining** | Costs of training employees on new technology or processes |
| **Reorganising plant layout** | Disruption and expense of rearranging production facilities |

### 13.2 Psychological Resistance (Inertia)

**Definition**: The emotional and psychological barriers to change — fear of the unknown, comfort with existing routines, and uncertainty about outcomes.

**The chain effect of resistance**:
```
Resistance to change
    → Less motivated employees
        → Lower productivity
            → Quality issues
                → Increased lead times and defects
                    → Lost customers
                        → Reduced revenue and profit
```

### 13.3 Managing Change

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1. Identify sources of change and assess need** | Understand why change is necessary; conduct cost-benefit analysis |
| **2. Communicate with employees** | Explain the reasons, benefits, and impact; get widespread support |
| **3. Use change agents** | Identify influential employees who champion the change |
| **4. Apply change models** | Use structured approaches (Lewin's 3-step model, Kotter's 8-step model) |

> **Apple**: When Apple transitioned from Intel chips to its own M-series chips (2020-2023), it required massive retraining of engineers, new software development, and convincing developers to adapt. Apple managed this by communicating the long-term benefits (better performance, lower power consumption) and providing extensive developer support.

## 15. Sequencing & Scheduling

### 15.1 Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sequencing** | The order in which activities are performed in the operations process |
| **Scheduling** | The length of time each activity takes |

### 15.2 Gantt Charts

**Definition**: A visual timeline showing activities, their order, and their duration — displayed as horizontal bars on a time scale.

**Advantages**:
- Shows concurrent (parallel) activities
- Identifies bottlenecks (tasks that delay everything downstream)
- Easy to communicate to team members
- Visual representation of progress

**Limitations**:
- Complex projects become difficult to read
- Doesn't show dependencies between tasks clearly
- Doesn't identify the critical path

### 15.3 Critical Path Analysis (CPA)

**Definition**: A technique that shows what needs to be done, how long each task takes, and in what order tasks must be performed — identifying the **critical path** (longest path through the network).

**Key Terms**:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Critical path** | The longest path through the network — determines the minimum project duration |
| **Critical tasks** | Tasks on the critical path — CANNOT be delayed without delaying the entire project |
| **Float (Slack)** | The amount of time a non-critical task can be delayed without affecting the project end date |
| **ES** | Earliest Start — earliest time a task can begin |
| **EF** | Earliest Finish — earliest time a task can end (ES + duration) |
| **LS** | Latest Start — latest time a task can start without delaying the project |
| **LF** | Latest Finish — latest time a task can end without delaying the project |

### 15.4 How to Calculate Critical Path

**Step-by-step process**:

1. **List all tasks** with their durations and dependencies
2. **Draw network diagram** showing task dependencies (arrows show order)
3. **Forward pass**: Calculate ES and EF for each task (start from the beginning)
4. **Backward pass**: Calculate LS and LF for each task (start from the end)
5. **Calculate Float**: Float = LS - ES (or LF - EF)
6. **Identify Critical Path**: Tasks with **zero float** = critical path
7. **Determine project duration**: The critical path duration = minimum time to complete all tasks

### 15.5 Worked Example: Critical Path Analysis

**Scenario**: A business is launching a new product with the following tasks:

| Task | Description | Duration (days) | Depends On |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Market research | 3 | None |
| B | Design product | 5 | A |
| C | Source suppliers | 4 | A |
| D | Build prototype | 4 | B |
| E | Test prototype | 3 | C |
| F | Final production | 2 | D, E |

**Step 1 — Network Diagram**:

```
        ┌──── B (5) ──── D (4) ────┐
        │                           │
A (3) ──┤                           ├── F (2) ── END
        │                           │
        └──── C (4) ──── E (3) ────┘
```

**Step 2 — Forward Pass** (calculate ES and EF):

| Task | ES | EF (ES + Duration) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 3 |
| B | 3 | 8 |
| C | 3 | 7 |
| D | 8 | 12 |
| E | 7 | 10 |
| F | 12 | 14 |

**Step 3 — Backward Pass** (calculate LS and LF):

| Task | LF | LS (LF - Duration) |
|---|---|---|
| F | 14 | 12 |
| D | 12 | 8 |
| E | 12 | 9 |
| B | 8 | 3 |
| C | 9 | 5 |
| A | 3 | 0 |

**Step 4 — Calculate Float**:

| Task | LS | ES | Float (LS - ES) | Critical? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 0 | **0** | ✅ YES |
| B | 3 | 3 | **0** | ✅ YES |
| C | 5 | 3 | 2 | ❌ No (2 days float) |
| D | 8 | 8 | **0** | ✅ YES |
| E | 9 | 7 | 2 | ❌ No (2 days float) |
| F | 12 | 12 | **0** | ✅ YES |

**Step 5 — Identify Critical Path**:

```
CRITICAL PATH: A → B → D → F = 3 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 14 DAYS
```

**Key insights**:
- **Minimum project duration**: 14 days
- **Critical tasks** (A, B, D, F): Cannot be delayed — any delay extends the project
- **Non-critical tasks** (C, E): Have 2 days float each — can be delayed up to 2 days without affecting the project end date

**What if Task C is delayed by 3 days?**
- Task C now finishes on day 10 (instead of 7)
- Task E starts on day 10 and finishes on day 13
- Task F must wait until day 13 (instead of day 12)
- **Project delayed by 1 day** — new duration = 15 days
- The critical path changes because C's float was exceeded
