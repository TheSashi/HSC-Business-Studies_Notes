# Operations — HSC Final Notes

> **Module: Operations** | Complete revision document for the HSC exam.
> Structured to the four syllabus sections: Role → Influences → Processes → Strategies.

## 1. Role of Operations Management

**Definition**: Operations refers to the business processes of **transformation** and **value adding**.

- **Transformation**: converting **inputs** into **outputs**
- **Value adding**: creating extra value as inputs become outputs. In **manufacturing** this is physical change; in **services** it is the application of knowledge and skills

### 1.1 Strategic Role of Operations

Operations contributes to the strategic direction of the business through **two generic strategies**:

| Strategy | Definition | How it is achieved |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost leadership** | Having the **lowest costs** and being the most price-competitive while remaining profitable | Economies of scale, automation, bulk buying, eliminating waste, standardisation, global sourcing |
| **Goods/services differentiation** | Distinguishing goods or services from competitors | **Goods**: vary features, quality, augmented features (warranty, delivery). **Services**: vary the amount of time, level of expertise, qualifications of staff |

> **Exam link**: Almost every Operations extended response should end by linking back to **cost leadership** or **differentiation**. That is the strategic role.

### 1.2 Goods vs Services

| Goods | Services |
|---|---|
| **Tangible** — can be stored | **Intangible** — produced and consumed simultaneously |
| Standardised or customised | Standardised (fast food) or customised (legal advice) |
| Quality measured by durability and fitness for purpose | Quality measured by professionalism and reliability |

**Classification of goods:**

- **Pure goods** — no service component (e.g. tinned food)
- **Pure services** — no goods component (e.g. financial advice)
- **Goods with a service component** — (e.g. a car with a warranty)

### 1.3 Interdependence with Other Key Business Functions

| Function | Relationship with Operations |
|---|---|
| **Marketing** | Marketing provides demand forecasts and customer preferences → operations modifies production. Operations produces the goods → marketing sells them |
| **Finance** | Finance provides budgets for equipment, technology and inputs. Operations controls costs, which drives profitability |
| **Human Resources** | HR recruits and trains production staff. Operations determines skill needs and staffing levels. Productivity depends directly on workforce quality |

## 2. Influences on Operations

| Influence | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Globalisation** | Removal of trade barriers; operations structured around global production facilities. **Opportunity**: global markets, economies of scale, global sourcing. **Threat**: intensified competition |
| **Technology** | Increased efficiency, reduced costs, consistent quality, higher productivity. Includes both **administrative** and **processing** technology |
| **Quality expectations** | **Goods**: design, fitness for purpose, durability. **Services**: professionalism, reliability, level of customisation. Failure → dissatisfaction, returns, damaged reputation |
| **Cost-based competition** | Pressure to lower production costs. Methods: eliminate waste, standardise, high volume, automate, economies of scale, bulk buying |
| **Government policies** | Changes in taxation, industrial relations, environmental regulation, training requirements — all create **compliance costs** |
| **Legal regulation** | WHS Act, Fair Work Act, Environmental Protection Act, Competition and Consumer Act, Food Safety Act. **Compliance costs** are the expenses of meeting these requirements |
| **Environmental sustainability** | Operating without compromising resources for future generations. Methods: minimise waste, recycle, reduce carbon footprint |

### 2.1 Corporate Social Responsibility

**CSR** = open and accountable business actions based on respect for people, community, society and the environment.

> **The triple bottom line** = financial **+** social **+** environmental performance.

> **Confusing pair — Legal compliance vs Ethical responsibility**
> **Legal compliance** = obeying the **letter of the law** — the minimum required.
> **Ethical responsibility** = acting in the **spirit of the law** — doing what is right even where no law compels it.
> A business can be fully legally compliant and still be criticised as unethical.

**The cost of CSR**: Environmentally sustainable practices often cost more in the short term (new equipment, sustainable inputs, waste treatment), which conflicts with **cost leadership**. The counter-argument is long-term reputation, customer loyalty and avoided regulatory penalties.

## 3. Operations Processes

```
INPUTS  →  TRANSFORMATION PROCESSES  →  OUTPUTS
```

### 3.1 Inputs

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformed resources** | The inputs **changed** by the process | **Materials** (raw materials, intermediate goods), **Information** (internal and external), **Customers** (where the customer themselves is changed, e.g. a haircut) |
| **Transforming resources** | The inputs that **carry out** the change | **Human resources** (skills, training, motivation), **Facilities** (plant, machinery, factory layout) |

> **Exam trap**: When a question mentions **customised products**, the key point is that **customers become a transformed resource** — they enter the process and shape it directly.

### 3.2 Transformation Processes — The Four Vs

| V | What it means | High | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | How much is produced | Efficient, automated, low unit cost, capital intensive | Manual, skilled labour, high unit cost |
| **Variety** | Range of products offered | Complex, flexible, expensive, process layout | Simple, efficient, standardised, product layout |
| **Variation in demand** | How much demand fluctuates | Need flexible resources, accurate forecasting, casual staff | Stable planning, consistent resource use |
| **Visibility** | Degree of customer contact | Skilled staff, responsive, quality interaction matters | Speed, consistency, cost control |

### 3.3 Technology, Task Design and Process Layout

**Technology**

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Leading edge** | The most advanced technology available — new, gives competitive advantage, but untested and expensive |
| **Established** | Proven technology that has been widely adopted — reliable and cheaper, but no advantage over competitors |

Benefits: faster production, fewer errors, consistent quality, long-term labour cost savings.

**Task design**: The process of classifying job activities so employees can perform them easily.
Steps: define the job → analyse it into duties → allocate difficulty and time → match to awards → articulate through **job descriptors**.

**Process layout types**

| Type | Use | Characteristics | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Process (functional) layout** | High variety, low volume | Equipment grouped by **function**; flexible; slower | Hospital, bank, printery |
| **Product layout** | High volume, standardised | **Assembly line**; consistent quality; inflexible | Car factory, Foxconn |
| **Fixed position layout** | Large, immovable products | Workers and equipment come **to the site** | Construction, shipbuilding |
| **Office layout** | Information flow | Arranged for communication and workflow | Open-plan offices |

### 3.4 Monitoring, Controlling and Improvement

| Stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Monitoring** | Measuring **actual** performance against **planned** performance using KPIs |
| **Controlling** | Assessing KPIs against targets, **investigating variances**, and taking **corrective action** |
| **Improvement** | The systematic reduction of inefficiencies, waste and bottlenecks |

**Common operations KPIs**: lead times, inventory turnover, defect rates, capacity utilisation, unit costs, waste levels, warranty claims.

**Areas of improvement**: time, process flows, quality, cost, efficiency.

> **Continuous improvement** = an ongoing commitment to achieving perfection. Requires **staff involvement** at every level — it cannot be imposed from the top alone.

> **Confusing term — Bottleneck**: The point in a process with the **lowest capacity**, which limits the output of the *entire* system. Improving anything other than the bottleneck does not increase total output.

### 3.5 Outputs

| Output element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Customer service** | How well the business meets and exceeds customer expectations |
| **Warranties** | A promise to repair or replace faulty goods. **Warranty claims** are a key source of feedback about quality failures — they signal where the transformation process is going wrong |

### 3.6 Sequencing and Scheduling

> **Definitions — learn these exact wordings**
> **Sequencing** refers to the **order** in which activities in the operations process occur.
> **Scheduling** refers to the **length of time** activities take within the operations process.

#### Gantt Charts

**Gantt chart**: outlines the activities that need to be performed, **the order** in which they should be performed, and **how long** each activity is expected to take.

```
Task          Week 1   Week 2   Week 3   Week 4   Week 5   Week 6
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Shop refit    ████████████████
Purchase      ─────────────────████████████
  stock
Staff         ─────────────────████████
  training
Marketing     ─────────────────────────████████████
Opening       ───────────────────────────────────────────█
```

**Advantages of Gantt charts:**

1. Show which activities can be performed **concurrently** (at the same time)
2. Identify **bottlenecks** and scheduling conflicts
3. Easy to communicate the plan to staff and stakeholders
4. Show total project duration at a glance
5. Allow progress to be tracked against plan

#### Critical Path Analysis (CPA)

**Critical path analysis**: shows **what needs to be done**, **how long tasks take**, and **what order** is necessary to complete those tasks.

> **The critical path is the shortest time to complete all of the tasks.**

> **How this actually works — read this once and it will never confuse you again.**
> A CPA diagram contains several possible **paths** from start to finish. You add up the durations along each path.
> - The **longest** path is the **critical path**.
> - Because every task must be finished, that longest path *is* the **shortest possible time** in which the whole project can be completed.
>
> So "longest path" and "shortest completion time" are **the same number** viewed two ways. The path is longest; the project time it produces is the shortest achievable. Both phrasings are correct — don't let them trip you up.

**Key CPA terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Critical path** | The sequence of activities that determines the total project duration |
| **Critical activity** | An activity **on** the critical path — it **cannot be delayed** without delaying the whole project |
| **Float (slack)** | The amount of time a non-critical activity **can** be delayed without delaying the project. Critical activities have **zero float** |
| **Concurrent activities** | Activities that can be carried out **at the same time** |
| **Dependency** | An activity that cannot start until another finishes |

> **Worked example — Critical Path Analysis**
>
> | Activity | Duration (days) | Must follow |
> |---|---|---|
> | A | 3 | — |
> | B | 4 | — |
> | C | 2 | A |
> | D | 5 | B |
> | E | 3 | C and D |
>
> **Network:**
> ```
>            A (3)        C (2)
>      ┌──────────►○──────────►┐
>  START                        ├──►  E (3)  ──► FINISH
>      └──────────►○──────────►┘
>            B (4)        D (5)
> ```
>
> **Step 1 — list every path and total it:**
> - Path 1: A → C → E = 3 + 2 + 3 = **8 days**
> - Path 2: B → D → E = 4 + 5 + 3 = **12 days**
>
> **Step 2 — identify the critical path:** the longest path is **B → D → E**.
>
> **Step 3 — state the project duration:** the shortest time to complete all tasks is **12 days**.
>
> **Step 4 — calculate float on the non-critical path:**
> Path A→C→E takes 8 days but has 12 days available, so **A and C each have 4 days of float**. They can be delayed up to 4 days without delaying the opening.
>
> **Answering the question "which task determines when E can be performed?"** → **D**, because E cannot begin until both C (finished day 5) and D (finished day 9) are complete. The later of the two — D — is the constraint.

**How CPA assists the transformation process:**

- Identifies which activities are **critical**, so management allocates resources there first
- Reveals **float**, allowing staff and equipment to be redeployed from non-critical tasks
- Reduces **lead time** by showing which activities can run **concurrently**
- Improves **dependability** by producing a realistic completion date
- Supports the **speed** performance objective

> **Gantt vs CPA — the distinction examiners look for**
> A **Gantt chart** shows activities against a **timeline** — it is best for *visualising* schedule and progress.
> **CPA** shows **dependencies** and identifies which activities **cannot be delayed** — it is best for *managing* time and resources.

## 4. Operations Strategies

### 4.1 Performance Objectives

| Objective | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Quality** | **Design quality** (how well it matches consumer understanding of what it should do) + **conformance quality** (how well it meets specifications) + **quality of service** |
| **Speed** | Fast response to market changes; reduced **lead times** and waiting times |
| **Dependability** | Consistency and reliability — the product works as promised, and delivery arrives when promised |
| **Flexibility** | The ability to adjust production to change quickly — in volume, product or delivery |
| **Customisation** | Creating individualised products to meet specific customer needs; **mass customisation** delivers this at volume |
| **Cost** | Minimising expenses so the business can compete on price while remaining profitable |

> **Confusing pair — Speed vs Dependability**
> **Speed** = how *fast* you deliver. **Dependability** = how *reliably* you deliver **when you said you would**.
> A business can be fast but undependable (quick sometimes, late others), or slow but perfectly dependable.

### 4.2 New Product or Service Design and Development

**Two approaches:**

| Approach | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Consumer-driven** | Customer preferences and market research determine the design |
| **Technology-driven** | Innovation and new technology enable products consumers had not asked for |

**Steps**: Market research → Design and prototype → Testing → Refinement → Production and launch

**Key considerations**: supply chain (do we need new suppliers?), quality (can standards be maintained?), capacity (do we need new resources or technology?), cost.

**Service design**:

- **Explicit service** — the **tangible** parts: the time taken, the expertise applied
- **Implicit service** — the **intangible** parts: the feeling of wellbeing, status, comfort
- Cost is determined **after** both elements are specified

### 4.3 Supply Chain Management

**SCM** = the integration and management of the flow of supplies from inputs, through transformation, to outputs.

**Sourcing** = deciding which suppliers to use. Factors: consumer demand, quality of inputs, flexibility and timeliness, cost.

**Trends in sourcing:**

- **Supplier rationalisation** — reducing the number of suppliers to gain better terms and consistency
- **Backwards vertical integration** — acquiring your own supplier
- **Cost minimisation** — moving sourcing offshore
- Building **flexible and responsive** supply chains

**Global sourcing** = purchasing inputs without being constrained by location.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Lower costs | Quality risk |
| Reduced lead times (if well managed) | Impact on domestic businesses and jobs |
| Access to materials unavailable domestically | Communication, cultural and time-zone barriers |
| Access to specialist expertise | Longer, more fragile supply lines |

**E-commerce** = using the internet in supply chain management. **E-procurement** systems automate ordering. **B2B** (business to business — automatic restocking between firms) and **B2C** (business to consumer — direct sales).

**Logistics** = distribution, transportation, storage (warehousing and distribution centres), materials handling and packaging.

### 4.4 Outsourcing

**Outsourcing** = the use of external providers to perform business activities — either **domestic** or **global**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cost savings | Communication and language barriers |
| Improved efficiency | Loss of direct quality control |
| Access to specialist skills | Security and confidentiality risks |
| Focus on core business | Dependency on the partner |
| Increased accountability through contracts | Loss of corporate memory and in-house skill |
| Simplification of the business | Reputation affected by the partner's conduct |

### 4.5 Technology

| Type | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Leading edge** | Newest available; competitive advantage; expensive and untested |
| **Established** | Proven and widely used; reliable and cheaper; no differentiation |

Key applications: **CAD** (computer-aided design), **CAM** (computer-aided manufacture), **robotics**, **automation**.

### 4.6 Inventory Management

> **Critical exam distinction**
> If the question asks about inventory **MANAGEMENT** → answer **Holding stock (JIC)** or **Just in Time (JIT)**.
> If the question asks about a strategy to **VALUE** inventory, or an inventory **VALUATION** method → answer **FIFO** or **LIFO**.
> Mixing these up is one of the most common ways students lose marks in this topic.

#### Holding Stock — "Just in Case" (JIC)

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can always meet customer demand | **Holding costs**: storage, insurance, theft, spoilage, security |
| Alternatives available if an item is out of stock | Capital tied up in stock that could be used elsewhere |
| Reduced lead times for customers | Risk of **obsolescence** (stock becoming outdated) |
| Immediate revenue from available stock | Damage and deterioration |
| Bulk-buying cost savings | Requires warehouse space |

#### Just in Time (JIT)

Produce or order **only** what is needed, when it is needed.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Greatly reduced storage costs | Lead time increases when demand varies unexpectedly |
| No theft or damage risk from stored stock | Total dependence on supplier reliability |
| No obsolescence | A single delivery failure can **shut down production** |
| Frees working capital | Loses bulk-buying discounts |

#### Inventory Valuation — FIFO, LIFO and Weighted Average

| Method | Definition |
|---|---|
| **FIFO** (First In First Out) | Assumes the **oldest** stock is sold first |
| **LIFO** (Last In First Out) | Assumes the **newest** stock is sold first |
| **Weighted average** | Uses the average cost of all inventory; **smooths** price fluctuations |

**The decision table — memorise this:**

| | **Understate / minimise profit** (to reduce tax) | **Overstate / maximise profit** (for potential investors) |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost of inventory is increasing** | **LIFO** | **FIFO** |
| **Cost of inventory is decreasing** | **FIFO** | **LIFO** |

> **Worked example — FIFO vs LIFO with rising costs**
>
> **Purchases:**
> | Date | Units | Cost per unit | Total |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 Jan | 100 | $10 | $1,000 |
> | 1 Feb | 100 | $12 | $1,200 |
> | 1 Mar | 100 | $14 | $1,400 |
> | **Total** | **300** | | **$3,600** |
>
> **150 units are sold at $20 each → Sales revenue = $3,000**
>
> **FIFO** (oldest sold first): COGS = (100 × $10) + (50 × $12) = 1,000 + 600 = **$1,600**
> Closing stock = (50 × $12) + (100 × $14) = 600 + 1,400 = **$2,000**
> **Gross profit = 3,000 − 1,600 = $1,400**
>
> **LIFO** (newest sold first): COGS = (100 × $14) + (50 × $12) = 1,400 + 600 = **$2,000**
> Closing stock = (50 × $12) + (100 × $10) = 600 + 1,000 = **$1,600**
> **Gross profit = 3,000 − 2,000 = $1,000**
>
> **Weighted average**: $3,600 ÷ 300 units = **$12 per unit**
> COGS = 150 × 12 = **$1,800** → **Gross profit = $1,200**
>
> **Conclusion**: With **rising** costs, **FIFO reports the higher profit** ($1,400) — chosen to impress investors. **LIFO reports the lower profit** ($1,000) — chosen to reduce tax. Weighted average sits between the two. Note COGS + closing stock = $3,600 in every method: the total cost is the same, only its *allocation* differs.

**EOQ (Economic Order Quantity)**: the optimal order quantity that **minimises total inventory costs** — balancing **ordering costs** (which fall as you order less often in bigger batches) against **holding costs** (which rise as you store more).

**LIFO note**: LIFO is permitted for HSC analysis but is **not permitted under Australian Accounting Standards** for external financial reporting. Answer the question as asked, using the table above.

### 4.7 Quality Management

| Approach | Key feature |
|---|---|
| **Quality control** | **Inspection** at various points in production. Define standards, test against them, take corrective action. **Detects** defects *after* they occur |
| **Quality assurance** | A **system** designed to **prevent** defects — "right the first time." Certified against external standards such as **ISO 9000** |
| **Total Quality Management (TQM)** | Holistic — **every employee** is responsible for quality; the total business is managed for quality. Elements: customer focus, continuous improvement, employee empowerment |
| **Six Sigma** | Data-driven; targets **3.4 defects per million** opportunities. Uses **DMAIC**: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control |
| **Kaizen** | Continuous **incremental** improvement involving all employees; eliminating waste (**muda**) |
| **Quality circles** | Small groups of employees who meet regularly to identify and solve quality problems |

> **Confusing pair — Quality control vs Quality assurance**
> **Control** = inspecting output to **find** defects (reactive, after the fact).
> **Assurance** = designing the **process** so defects don't happen (proactive, before the fact).

### 4.8 Overcoming Resistance to Change

**Two sources of resistance:**

| Source | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Financial costs** | Purchasing new equipment, **redundancy payouts**, retraining costs, reorganising plant layout |
| **Psychological (inertia)** | Fear and uncertainty → less motivated staff → lower productivity → quality issues → increased lead times and defects → lost customers |

> **Inertia** = the tendency of people and organisations to **keep doing what they have always done** and resist any change to it.

**Managing change effectively:**

1. Identify the sources of change and **assess the need** for it
2. **Communicate** with employees and build widespread support
3. Use **change agents** — people who champion and drive the change
4. Apply **change models** (e.g. Lewin's unfreeze → change → refreeze)

### 4.9 Global Factors

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Global sourcing** | Sourcing inputs anywhere in the world for cost or quality advantage |
| **Economies of scale** | Higher volumes → **lower per-unit costs**, through capital investment, technology, global branding and centralised training |
| **Scanning and learning** | Actively studying and adopting **global best practice** from other markets and competitors |
| **Research and development** | Investing in leading-edge technologies; global expertise increases the opportunity base |

## 5. Key Case Study — Apple

| Topic | Example |
|---|---|
| **Inputs** | *Transformed*: screens, chips, circuitry, customer feedback. *Transforming*: Foxconn factories, Chinese labour |
| **4 Vs** | High **volume** at Foxconn; wide **variety** (iPhone, iPad, Mac, Watch); **variation** risky (over/under ordering); high **visibility** (customer-driven design) |
| **Quality** | 5M iPhones returned (2013) — lack of training, $1.6bn cost; defective Apple Maps (2012); facial recognition failure on iPhone X (2018) |
| **Outsourcing** | Production to Foxconn: lower cost, expertise, economies of scale — but loss of quality control, security risks, cultural barriers |
| **Supply chain** | 785 suppliers across 31 countries; COVID delayed iPhone 12; chip shortage (2021); Tim Cook reduced suppliers and warehouses |
| **Cost objectives** | iPhone SE cheaper components; in-house chip development; Samsung→TSMC supplier switch; China→India production shift; purchased Intel's 5G division |
| **Global factors** | Components from USA, Korea, Taiwan, Europe, China; global branding; growing R&D investment |

## 6. Section IV & Short Answer Structures

### HSC Verb Frameworks

| Verb | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Identify / State** | Name it — no explanation needed |
| **Outline** | Sketch in general terms; answer + brief details |
| **Describe** | Features and characteristics; define + give 2 or more features |
| **Distinguish** | Differences between two things: define 1 → define 2 → state differences → link |
| **Explain** | Cause and effect: answer → how → why → link to goals |
| **Analyse** | Show the **relationship** and draw out **implications** |
| **Assess** | Make a **judgement based on criteria** |
| **Discuss** | Issues, advantages and disadvantages, then judgement |
| **Evaluate** | Weighted judgement supported by evidence |

### Worked Structure — "Assess the impact of customised products on the operations process" (Q21c)

**Assess** = make a judgement based on criteria.

```
1. Judgement sentence: "Customised products have a large impact on the
   operations process of [business]…" + define the operations process
2. Inputs:      Customers become a TRANSFORMED RESOURCE
3. Process:     Technology, task design and layout are all affected
4. Effect:      Flexible production needed, skilled workers, process layouts
5. Implication: What this means for the business
6. Restate the judgement
```

### Worked Structure — "Analyse the use of outsourcing in achieving the strategic role of Operations"

**Analyse** = show the relationship and draw out implications.

```
1. Define outsourcing
2. How does outsourcing impact operations? (cost reduction, access to expertise)
3. Effect: link to a PERFORMANCE OBJECTIVE (cost, quality, flexibility)
4. Implication: link to COST LEADERSHIP or DIFFERENTIATION
5. Use relational verbs: therefore, as a result, consequently, this means
```

### PEEL Structure

**P**oint → **E**xplain → **E**vidence (case study) → **L**ink back to the question

## 7. Common Exam Pitfalls

1. **Inventory MANAGEMENT (JIT/JIC) vs VALUATION (FIFO/LIFO)** — read the verb in the question.
2. **Critical path**: the longest path through the network gives the shortest possible project time. Both phrasings describe the same number.
3. **Sequencing = order. Scheduling = length of time.** Don't swap them.
4. **Quality control (detects) vs quality assurance (prevents).**
5. Always link back to **cost leadership** or **differentiation** — that is the strategic role.
6. **Transformed** resources are changed; **transforming** resources do the changing.
7. Customers can be **both** an input (transformed resource) and an output recipient.
8. Don't list influences — **explain the impact** on the operations process.
9. **Leading edge vs established** technology: the trade-off is advantage versus reliability and cost.
10. **Compliance costs** are the *expenses of meeting* legal requirements, not the fines for breaching them.

## 8. Glossary — Terms Students Mix Up

| Term | Plain meaning | Don't confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformed resources** | Inputs that are **changed** (materials, information, customers) | **Transforming resources** — those that *do* the changing |
| **Transforming resources** | Human resources and facilities | **Transformed resources** |
| **Sequencing** | The **order** of activities | **Scheduling** — the **duration** |
| **Critical path** | Longest path through the network | The *number of tasks* — it's about **time**, not count |
| **Float / slack** | Spare time a non-critical activity has | Critical activities have **zero** float |
| **Lead time** | Time between ordering and receiving | **Cycle time** — time to produce one unit |
| **Bottleneck** | The lowest-capacity point limiting total output | A general "delay" |
| **Quality control** | Inspecting to **find** defects | **Quality assurance** — preventing them |
| **Quality assurance** | System preventing defects; ISO 9000 | **TQM** — whole-of-business philosophy |
| **JIT** | Inventory **management** strategy | **FIFO/LIFO** — inventory **valuation** |
| **FIFO / LIFO** | Inventory **valuation** methods | **JIT/JIC** — inventory **management** |
| **EOQ** | Order size minimising total inventory cost | Reorder point (*when* to order) |
| **Obsolescence** | Stock becoming outdated and unsellable | Spoilage (physical deterioration) |
| **Outsourcing** | Using an **external provider** for an activity | **Offshoring** — moving it overseas (may still be in-house) |
| **Global sourcing** | Buying **inputs** from anywhere in the world | **Outsourcing** — contracting out an **activity** |
| **Economies of scale** | Lower **per-unit** cost at higher volume | Lower total cost (total cost still rises) |
| **Inertia** | Resistance to change from habit and fear | Financial resistance (cost-based) |
| **Compliance cost** | Expense of **meeting** legal requirements | Penalty for breaching them |
| **Cost leadership** | Competing by being the **lowest cost** | Lowest **price** — a low-cost firm may still charge a premium |
| **Design quality** | Matching consumer understanding of the product | **Conformance quality** — meeting the specification |
| **Explicit service** | Tangible service elements (time, expertise) | **Implicit service** — intangible feelings |
| **Triple bottom line** | Financial + social + environmental | Just "being green" |

## 9. Key Definitions Quick Reference

| Operations | The business process of transformation and value adding |

| Value adding | Creating extra value as inputs become outputs |
| Cost leadership | Having the lowest costs and being most price competitive while profitable |
| Differentiation | Distinguishing goods or services from those of competitors |
| Transformed resources | Inputs changed by the process — materials, information, customers |
