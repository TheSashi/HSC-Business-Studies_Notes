# Human Resources Management — HSC Final Notes

> **Module 7: Human Resources** | Complete revision document for the HSC Business Studies exam. Covers the role of HRM, influences, legal framework, the HR cycle, motivation theories, organisational structures, and exam techniques.

---

## 1. The Role of Human Resource Management

**Definition**: Human Resource Management (HRM) is the **strategic approach** to managing an organisation's most valued asset — its people. It involves recruiting, developing, rewarding, and retaining employees while working within a legal framework to achieve business objectives.

### 1.1 Core Functions of HRM

| Function | Description | Business Impact |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Recruitment & Selection** | Attracting and choosing the right candidates for roles | Ensures skilled workforce; reduces turnover costs |
| **Training & Development** | Building employee skills and capabilities | Improves productivity; supports innovation |
| **Performance Management** | Monitoring, evaluating, and improving employee output | Aligns individual goals with business objectives |
| **Remuneration & Rewards** | Designing competitive pay and benefit packages | Attracts talent; motivates high performance |
| **Employee Relations** | Managing the employer-employee relationship | Reduces disputes; improves workplace culture |
| **Legal Compliance** | Ensuring adherence to workplace laws and regulations | Avoids penalties; protects business reputation |
| **Workforce Planning** | Forecasting future staffing needs | Ensures business can meet demand |

### 1.2 The Strategic Role of HRM

HRM is no longer just an administrative function — it plays a **strategic, proactive role** in long-term business planning.

| Aspect | Reactive (Administrative) | Proactive (Strategic) |
|--------|---------------------------|----------------------|
| **Focus** | Day-to-day tasks (payroll, forms) | Long-term workforce planning |
| **Approach** | Responds to problems as they arise | Anticipates future needs |
| **Contribution** | Cost centre | Value-adding partner |
| **Planning** | Short-term, reactive | Long-term, aligned with business strategy |
| **Example** | Processing a resignation | Succession planning for key roles |

**Benefits of a strategic HR approach:**
- Builds a **highly qualified workforce** matched to current and future needs
- Ensures workforce is **relevant to organisational goals** and industry trends
- Supports **change management** during restructuring or growth
- Enhances **employer brand**, attracting top talent
- Reduces **staff turnover** and associated costs (recruitment, training losses)

> **Apple**: Apple's HR strategy is deeply integrated with its innovation goals. HR recruits for creativity and cross-collaboration, designs rigorous interview processes (2–7 rounds), and develops employees through internal mobility. This strategic alignment ensures Apple maintains its competitive edge through people.

### 1.3 Interdependence of HRM with Other Functions

HRM does not operate in isolation — it is **interdependent** with all other business functions.

| Function | Relationship with HRM | Example |
|----------|----------------------|---------|
| **Operations** | HR recruits and trains production staff; productivity depends on workforce skill and motivation | McDonald's trains crew in standardised procedures to ensure fast, consistent service |
| **Marketing** | HR recruits customer-facing staff; service quality depends on employee skills and attitude | Apple retail "Geniuses" are carefully selected and trained for customer experience |
| **Finance** | HR costs (wages, training, benefits) are major budget items; finance sets salary budgets | NAB's 12% staff reduction was a financial decision with massive HR implications |
| **Stakeholders** | HR must balance needs of employers, employees, unions, and government | Enterprise bargaining negotiations involve multiple stakeholder interests |

> **Exam tip:** Always demonstrate **interdependence** — show how a HR decision affects (and is affected by) operations, marketing, finance, and stakeholders. This is a key marking criterion.

## 2. Influences on Human Resource Management

HRM decisions are shaped by multiple internal and external factors.

### 2.1 Stakeholders Influencing HRM

| Stakeholder | Role in HRM | Key Actions |
|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Employers/Owners** | Set HR strategy, approve budgets, make final decisions on staffing | Define organisational structure, set remuneration levels, approve redundancy |
| **Employees** | Seek fair pay, safe conditions, career development, work-life balance | Negotiate through unions, participate in enterprise bargaining, voice concerns |
| **Employer Organisations** | Represent employer interests in industrial relations | Business Council of Australia (BCA), Australian Chamber of Commerce and Industry (ACCI) |
| **Trade Unions** | Represent employee interests, negotiate pay and conditions | Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU), shop stewards, collective bargaining |
| **Government** | Set and enforce the legal framework for employment | Fair Work Commission, Federal Court, Human Rights Commission, EOWA (now WGEA) |
| **Society** | Expectations around corporate social responsibility, diversity, ethics | Community expectations for fair treatment, environmental responsibility |

### 2.2 Legal Framework

The legal framework is the **most significant external influence** on HRM in Australia.

#### The National Fair Work System (Established 1 July 2009)

The Fair Work Act 2009 created a national system governing employment relationships across Australia.

**11 National Employment Standards (NES):**

| NES Entitlement | Details |
|-----------------|---------|
| **Maximum weekly hours** | 38 hours per week + reasonable additional hours |
| **Flexible working arrangements** | Right to request flexible hours (parents, carers, disability) |
| **Offers and requests to convert from casual to permanent** | Casual employees can request conversion after 12 months |
| **Parental leave** | 12 months unpaid + 24 weeks paid parental leave (shared between parents) |
| **Annual leave** | 4 weeks paid annual leave per year |
| **Personal/carer's leave** | 10 days paid personal/carer's leave per year |
| **Community service leave** | Paid leave for jury duty; unpaid for voluntary emergency management |
| **Long service leave** | Leave after long service (varies by state, typically 7–10 years) |
| **Public holidays** | Day off on public holidays; 1.5× pay if required to work |
| **Notice of termination** | Minimum notice periods based on length of service |
| **Redundancy pay** | Payments based on years of continuous service |

#### Modern Awards

- Industry-specific documents setting **minimum pay rates and conditions**
- Cover most employees not covered by an enterprise agreement
- Example: Fast Food Industry Award, Banking Industry Award
- Set by the Fair Work Commission after annual wage reviews
- Current national minimum wage: **$26.44 per hour** (as of 2024)

#### Employment Contracts

| Type | Description | Key Feature |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| **Modern Award** | Sets minimum terms for an industry/occupation | Safety net — cannot be undercut |
| **Enterprise Agreement** | Negotiated between employer and employees (or union) | Must pass the **BOOT test** (Better Off Overall Test) |
| **Individual Contract** | Direct agreement between employer and employee | Terms must be better than NES and Award minimums |

#### Common Law Duties

**Employer's duties to employees:**
- **Duty of care** — provide a safe workplace (physical AND psychological safety)
- Pay the agreed wage on time
- Provide the work as agreed in the contract
- Act in good faith

**Employee's duties to employer:**
- Obey lawful and reasonable instructions
- Work with reasonable skill and care
- Disclose relevant information to the employer
- Act in good faith and not breach confidentiality

#### Key Legislation

| Legislation | Purpose |
|-------------|---------|
| **Fair Work Act 2009** | National workplace relations — awards, enterprise agreements, unfair dismissal, right to bargain |
| **Work Health and Safety Act 2011** | Employer duty of care for physical and psychological safety; hazard identification, risk management |
| **Anti-Discrimination Acts** (federal & state) | Prohibit discrimination based on race, gender, age, disability, sexual orientation, religion |
| **Workers Compensation** | Mandatory insurance covering employees for work-related injuries and illnesses |
| **Equal Employment Opportunity (EEO)** | Ensures fair and non-discriminatory recruitment, promotion, and treatment |

### 2.3 Economic Influences

| Economic Factor | Impact on HRM |
|----------------|---------------|
| **Business cycle** | Expansion = more hiring; Recession = redundancies, hiring freezes |
| **Inflation** | Rising cost of living → pressure to increase wages; erodes real wages if stagnant |
| **Unemployment rate** | High unemployment = larger applicant pool; Low unemployment = skills shortage, higher wages |
| **Exchange rates** | Affects import costs → may influence staffing levels in import-dependent businesses |
| **Interest rates** | Affects business costs → may influence capacity to hire or invest in training |

### 2.4 Technological Influences

| Technology | Positive Impact | Negative Impact |
|------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Automation/AI** | Increases efficiency, reduces repetitive tasks | Causes job displacement, requires retraining |
| **HRIS (HR Information Systems)** | Streamlines payroll, leave, recruitment tracking | Requires investment and staff training |
| **Video conferencing** | Enables remote interviews, WFH, global teams | Reduced face-to-face interaction |
| **Online recruitment** | Wider applicant pool, faster hiring | Harder to assess cultural fit |
| **Social media** | Employer branding, talent sourcing | Privacy concerns, reputational risk |

> **NAB**: NAB automated 12% of its workforce roles through technology, but simultaneously created 2,000 new technology-focused positions. This demonstrates how technology both eliminates and creates jobs — requiring HR to manage transition through retraining and redeployment.

### 2.5 Social Influences

| Social Factor | Impact on HRM |
|---------------|---------------|
| **Feminisation of the workforce** | More women in the workforce → need for parental leave, flexible work, anti-discrimination policies |
| **Changing working patterns** | Shift from 9-5 to flexible, casual, gig economy work |
| **Work from home (WFH)** | COVID accelerated remote work → new management challenges, tech needs |
| **Manufacturing to service economy** | Decline in factory jobs; growth in service sector requiring different skills |
| **Aging workforce** | Older workers staying longer → succession planning, age discrimination awareness |
| **Cultural diversity** | Multicultural workforce → need for inclusive policies, anti-discrimination compliance |

### 2.6 Ethical and CSR Influences

| Factor | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Affirmative action** | Active recruitment of underrepresented groups | Targeting Indigenous employment, women in leadership |
| **Corporate Social Responsibility** | Volunteering programs, community engagement, ethical employment | BYD's commitment to fair labour in its supply chain |
| **Code of conduct** | Document outlining expected workplace behaviour | Anti-bullying, anti-harassment, whistleblowing protections |
| **Ethical recruitment** | Fair selection processes, transparent pay, no exploitation | Avoiding use of global contractors to circumvent labour laws |

## 3. Outsourcing and Contractors

### 3.1 Outsourcing HR Functions

**Definition**: Outsourcing is the practice of contracting external providers to perform specific business functions that were previously handled in-house.

**Reasons for outsourcing HR:**
- Access **specialist HR expertise** not available internally
- Allow management to **focus on core operations** and strategic goals
- Achieve **cost savings** (external provider may be more efficient)
- Access **latest HR technology** and best practices

**Risks of outsourcing HR:**
- Lack of understanding of **business culture** and internal dynamics
- Key relationships with employees must still be **developed internally**
- Loss of **direct control** over HR processes
- Potential **confidentiality and data security** risks
- Dependency on external provider — **continuity risk**

### 3.2 Contractors vs Employees

| Feature | Employees | Contractors |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **Tax** | Tax paid on behalf by employer (PAYG) | Responsible for own tax (ABN, GST) |
| **Superannuation** | Employer must pay super (currently 11.5%) | No super guaranteed by client |
| **Workers compensation** | Covered by employer's insurance | Must provide own insurance |
| **Leave entitlements** | Annual leave, sick leave, personal/carer's leave | No leave entitlements |
| **Legislation** | Covered by Fair Work Act, NES, WHS Act | Governed by contract law; limited govt legislation |
| **Control** | Employer directs how, when, where work is done | Contractor controls their own methods |
| **Integration** | Part of the organisation | External to the organisation |
| **Risk** | Employer bears financial risk | Contractor bears their own financial risk |

### 3.3 Domestic vs Global Contractors

| Feature | Domestic Contractors | Global Contractors |
|---------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **Location** | Within Australia | Offshore/international |
| **Labour laws** | Must comply with Australian law | May operate in countries with fewer labour protections |
| **Benefits** | Fresh ideas and perspectives from outside the organisation | Access to labour without minimum labour requirements or OH&S standards |
| **Focus** | Focus on essential/strategic activities | Cost minimisation |
| **Risks** | Loss of direct customer contact; diminished communication | Quality control issues; cultural differences; security issues; lack of legal remedies; high labour turnover |
| **Communication** | Same time zone, same language | Time zone differences; language barriers |
| **Example** | Australian marketing agency | Call centre in Philippines |

### 3.4 Forms of Outsourcing

| Form | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Process Outsourcing** | Outsourcing repetitive, routine tasks | Payroll processing, data entry |
| **Project Outsourcing** | Outsourcing specialised project-based work | HR strategy design, marketing campaigns, IT development, design work |

**Advantages of outsourcing generally:**
- Expand capacity and flexibility
- Improve quality through specialist expertise
- Save costs through economies of scale of the provider
- Access new networks and markets
- Conserve capital (avoid investing in in-house capability)

**Disadvantages of outsourcing:**
- Quality control issues — less direct oversight
- Cultural differences between organisations
- Security and confidentiality risks
- Lack of legal remedies if contract disputes arise
- High labour turnover in outsourced operations
- Loss of institutional knowledge

## 4. The HR Cycle: Acquisition

The HR cycle consists of four key stages: **Acquisition → Development → Maintenance → Separation**.

### 4.1 Overview of the HR Cycle

```
┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐
│ ACQUISITION  │────▶│ DEVELOPMENT  │────▶│ MAINTENANCE  │────▶│ SEPARATION   │
│              │     │              │     │              │     │              │
│ • Identify   │     │ • Training   │     │ • Pay &      │     │ • Retirement │
│   needs      │     │ • Dev programs│     │   benefits   │     │ • Resignation│
│ • Recruit    │     │ • Performance│     │ • Non-monetary│    │ • Redundancy │
│ • Select     │     │   appraisal  │     │ • Intrinsic  │     │ • Dismissal  │
│ • Induct     │     │              │     │   rewards    │     │              │
└──────────────┘     └──────────────┘     └──────────────┘     └──────────────┘
```

### 4.2 Step 1: Identifying HR Needs

Before recruiting, the business must determine **how many staff** are needed, **what skills** are required, and **when** they are needed.

**Methods for identifying needs:**

| Method | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Strategic plan analysis** | Align staffing with business growth objectives |
| **Workforce planning** | Forecast future demand vs current supply of labour |
| **Skills audit** | Assess current employee capabilities against required skills |
| **Turnover analysis** | Track resignation rates to predict replacement needs |
| **Budget analysis** | Determine how many staff the budget can support |
| **Operational planning** | Link staffing to production targets and demand forecasts |

### 4.3 Step 2: Job Description and Person Specification

| Document | Purpose | Contents |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **Job Description** | Describes the ROLE — what the job involves | Job title, duties/responsibilities, reporting lines, hours, location, salary range |
| **Person Specification** | Describes the IDEAL CANDIDATE — who should fill the role | Qualifications, experience, skills, competencies, personal attributes |

> **Apple**: Apple's job descriptions emphasise cross-collaboration, flexibility, adaptability, and problem-solving — reflecting its innovation-driven culture. They look for people who can work across teams and thrive in ambiguity.

### 4.4 Step 3: Recruitment

**Definition**: The process of attracting a pool of suitable candidates for a position.

| Method | Internal Recruitment | External Recruitment |
|--------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Sources** | Promotions, transfers, internal job boards, employee referrals | Seek, LinkedIn, company website, social media, recruitment agencies, job fairs |
| **Cost** | Lower — no advertising or agency fees | Higher — advertising, agency commissions |
| **Speed** | Faster — candidates already known | Slower — need to screen larger pool |
| **Impact on morale** | Motivates existing employees — shows career pathways | May demotivate internal candidates overlooked |
| **Fresh ideas** | Limited — same organisational culture | Brings new perspectives and experience |
| **Risk** | Limited talent pool; "inbreeding" of ideas | Unknown performance; longer adjustment period |
| **Knowledge** | Candidates know the business | Candidates need to learn the business |

### 4.5 Step 4: Selection

**Definition**: The process of choosing the most suitable candidate from the applicant pool.

| Selection Method | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------------|-------------|------------|---------------|
| **Interview** | Formal meeting to assess suitability | Allows assessment of communication, personality | Biased; stressful; may not predict job performance |
| **Psychometric testing** | Standardised tests of ability, personality, aptitude | Objective; predicts performance | Expensive; may not account for cultural factors |
| **Skills test** | Practical demonstration of job-related skills | Directly measures capability | Time-consuming; may not cover all aspects |
| **Referee checks** | Contacting previous employers for references | Verifies claims; provides external perspective | Referees may be biased; limited information |
| **Assessment centre** | Multiple methods combined (group exercises, presentations, role-plays) | Comprehensive assessment | Very expensive; time-intensive |
| **Trial period** | Temporary employment to assess fit | Low-risk; real-world assessment | Legal considerations; uncertainty for employee |

> **Apple**: Apple uses 2–7 interviews per candidate, assessing for cross-collaboration, flexibility, adaptability, and problem-solving. This rigorous process ensures cultural fit and capability alignment.

### 4.6 Step 5: Induction

**Definition**: The process of introducing a new employee to the organisation, their role, and the workplace.

| Induction Element | Description |
|-------------------|-------------|
| **Organisational overview** | Mission, values, structure, history, culture |
| **Policies and procedures** | Code of conduct, dress code, leave policies, social media policy |
| **WHS training** | Emergency procedures, hazard identification, safe work practices |
| **Facilities tour** | Office/factory layout, amenities, parking, emergency exits |
| **Entitlements** | Pay schedule, leave entitlements, superannuation, benefits |
| **Role-specific training** | Job duties, tools/systems, reporting lines |
| **Mentor/buddy assignment** | Pairing with an experienced employee for ongoing support |

**Benefits of effective induction:**
- Reduces new employee anxiety and uncertainty
- Speeds up time to full productivity
- Improves early job satisfaction and reduces early turnover
- Ensures WHS compliance from day one
- Sets clear expectations for performance and behaviour

## 5. The HR Cycle: Development

### 5.1 Training and Development

**Definition**: Activities designed to improve employee knowledge, skills, and capabilities to enhance both individual and organisational performance.

#### On-the-Job Training

| Method | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Mentoring** | Experienced employee guides a less experienced one | Senior McDonald's crew member training new staff on procedures |
| **Coaching** | Ongoing feedback and guidance from a supervisor | Manager coaching a sales team member on customer interaction |
| **Job rotation** | Moving employees between different roles/tasks | BYD rotating engineers across R&D, production, and quality assurance |
| **Job shadowing** | Observing an experienced worker without direct responsibility | New Apple retail employee shadowing a senior "Genius" |
| **Apprenticeships** | Formal structured training combining work and study | Electrician apprenticeship combining TAFE study with on-site work |
| **Delegation** | Assigning increased responsibilities to develop skills | Manager delegating project leadership to a team member |

**Advantages of on-the-job training:**
- Practical and immediately relevant to the role
- Lower cost — no external course fees
- Learn while earning (productive during training)
- Tailored to specific workplace needs

**Disadvantages of on-the-job training:**
- Can disrupt productive work for the trainer
- May reinforce bad habits if trainer is poor
- Less structured — may miss important theoretical knowledge
- Trainer may lack teaching skills

#### Off-the-Job Training

| Method | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Conferences/seminars** | Industry events for knowledge sharing | HR manager attending an employment law conference |
| **TAFE/university courses** | Formal qualifications | Employee completing a Diploma of Management through TAFE |
| **External workshops** | Specialist skill development | Leadership workshop, Microsoft Office training |
| **Online learning** | E-learning platforms, webinars | LinkedIn Learning, Coursera, internal LMS |
| **Study leave** | Paid time off for formal education | NAB offering study assistance for employees pursuing further qualifications |

**Advantages of off-the-job training:**
- Expert instruction from qualified trainers
- Dedicated learning environment — fewer distractions
- Exposure to best practices and external perspectives
- Formal qualifications recognised industry-wide

**Disadvantages of off-the-job training:**
- Expensive — course fees, travel, accommodation
- Time away from productive work
- May not be immediately relevant to the specific role
- Knowledge transfer back to workplace not guaranteed

### 5.2 Benefits of Training

| Stakeholder | Benefits |
|-------------|----------|
| **Employee** | Increased skills, career advancement, higher pay potential, job satisfaction, confidence |
| **Business** | Higher productivity, improved quality, reduced errors, lower turnover, competitive advantage |
| **Economy** | More skilled workforce, higher GDP, reduced unemployment benefits, innovation |
| **Customer** | Better service quality, faster response times, more knowledgeable staff, higher satisfaction |

### 5.3 Performance Appraisal

**Definition**: A formal, systematic evaluation of an employee's job performance and contribution to the organisation.

**Purpose of performance appraisals:**
- Identify employee **strengths** and areas for improvement
- Set **goals and KPIs** for the coming period
- Provide **feedback** to guide development
- Form the basis for **promotions, pay increases**, and career planning
- Identify **training needs** across the organisation
- Support **documentation** for disciplinary or termination processes

**Performance appraisal methods:**

| Method | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|-------------|------------|---------------|
| **Rating scales** | Rate performance on predetermined criteria (1-5) | Standardised; easy to compare | Subjective; may not capture full picture |
| **Self-assessment** | Employee evaluates their own performance | Promotes reflection; increases ownership | May be biased; hard to compare across staff |
| **360-degree feedback** | Feedback from supervisor, peers, subordinates, and self | Comprehensive; multiple perspectives | Time-consuming; may include personal bias |
| **Management by objectives (MBO)** | Manager and employee agree on specific objectives | Clear targets; aligned with goals | Can be rigid; may miss broader contributions |
| **Balanced scorecard** | Measures 4 perspectives: financial, customer, internal processes, learning & growth | Holistic view of performance | Complex to implement |

**360-Degree Feedback — Detail:**

```
                    ┌──────────────┐
                    │  SUPERVISOR  │
                    │   FEEDBACK   │
                    └──────┬───────┘
                           │
         ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
         │                 │                 │
         ▼                 ▼                 ▼
┌──────────────┐  ┌──────────────┐  ┌──────────────┐
│    PEER      │  │   EMPLOYEE   │  │  SUBORDINATE │
│   FEEDBACK   │──│   (SELF)     │──│   FEEDBACK   │
└──────────────┘  └──────────────┘  └──────────────┘
```

**Balanced Scorecard — 4 Perspectives:**

| Perspective | Key Question | HR Metrics |
|-------------|-------------|------------|
| **Financial** | How do we look to shareholders? | Labour costs per unit, revenue per employee, training ROI |
| **Customer** | How do customers see us? | Customer satisfaction scores, service quality ratings |
| **Internal Business Processes** | What must we excel at? | Time to fill vacancies, error rates, productivity measures |
| **Learning & Growth** | Can we continue to improve? | Training hours per employee, employee satisfaction, innovation rate |

## 6. The HR Cycle: Maintenance

**Definition**: Maintaining employees involves keeping them motivated, satisfied, and committed to the organisation through various rewards and benefits.

### 6.1 Monetary (Financial) Rewards

| Reward Type | Description | Example |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| **Base salary/wages** | Fixed regular payment for work performed | Annual salary for a manager; hourly wage for a retail worker |
| **Commission** | Payment based on sales performance | Real estate agent earning percentage of property sale price |
| **Piece rate** | Payment per unit produced | Factory worker paid per item manufactured |
| **Bonuses** | Additional payment for achieving targets | NAB offering performance bonuses for meeting quarterly KPIs |
| **Profit sharing** | Employees share in business profits | Coles staff profit-sharing program |
| **Shared ownership/ESOPs** | Employees given shares in the company | Apple's Employee Stock Purchase Plan (ESPP) |
| **Fringe benefits** | Non-cash benefits with monetary value | Company car, health insurance, gym membership, housing allowance |

**Pros of monetary rewards:**
- Directly linked to performance — incentivises effort
- Measurable and transparent
- Attracts and retains talent in competitive markets

**Cons of monetary rewards:**
- Can create unhealthy competition between employees
- May lead to short-term focus at expense of long-term goals
- High cost to the business — affects profitability
- Diminishing returns — extra money may not motivate beyond a certain level

### 6.2 Non-Monetary Rewards

| Reward Type | Description | Example |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| **Job variety** | Performing different tasks and challenges | Job rotation at BYD so engineers work across projects |
| **Flexible hours** | Control over when work is performed | Flextime, compressed work week |
| **Status/recognition** | Formal or informal acknowledgment of contribution | Employee of the Month, public recognition, job title upgrade |
| **Autonomy** | Independence and control over how work is done | Allowing marketing team to set their own creative direction |
| **Job sharing** | Two employees share one full-time role | Two part-time employees each working 3 days in one position |
| **Telecommuting/WFH** | Working from home or remote location | Post-COVID, many businesses offer permanent WFH options |
| **Professional development** | Opportunities for growth and learning | Paid conference attendance, study leave, mentoring programs |
| **Improved working environment** | Comfortable, well-equipped workspace | Modern office design, ergonomic furniture, break rooms |

**Pros of non-monetary rewards:**
- Often lower cost than salary increases
- Can be more motivating for knowledge workers
- Improve work-life balance → reduce absenteeism and turnover
- Enhance employer brand

**Cons of non-monetary rewards:**
- Difficult to measure effectiveness
- May not satisfy employees who prioritise financial compensation
- One-size-fits-all approaches may not work
- Can be perceived as a substitute for fair pay

### 6.3 Intrinsic Rewards

**Definition**: Internal, psychological rewards that come from the work itself — satisfaction derived from doing meaningful, challenging, or enjoyable work.

| Intrinsic Reward | Description |
|-----------------|-------------|
| **Sense of achievement** | Pride in completing a challenging project or meeting a goal |
| **Personal growth** | Feeling of development and learning new skills |
| **Purpose** | Understanding how one's work contributes to a larger mission |
| **Mastery** | Developing expertise and becoming excellent at something |
| **Autonomy** | Having control and ownership over one's work |

> **Herzberg connection**: Intrinsic rewards align with Herzberg's "motivators" — achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, and personal growth. These factors genuinely motivate employees, whereas monetary rewards (hygiene factors) only prevent dissatisfaction.

### 6.4 Remuneration Packages

A **remuneration package** combines monetary and non-monetary rewards into a comprehensive offer:

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Base salary** | Fixed annual or hourly pay |
| **Superannuation** | Mandatory employer contribution (currently 11.5%) |
| **Bonus/commission** | Performance-linked payments |
| **Fringe benefits** | Car, health insurance, phone, laptop |
| **Leave entitlements** | Annual leave, personal leave, long service leave |
| **Flexible work** | WFH, flextime, compressed week |
| **Professional development** | Training budgets, study leave, conference attendance |
| **Other perks** | Gym membership, employee discounts, wellness programs |

## 7. The HR Cycle: Separation

**Definition**: Separation occurs when an employee leaves the organisation, either voluntarily or involuntarily.

### 7.1 Voluntary Separation

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Retirement** | Employee leaves upon reaching retirement age | Long-serving manager retiring after 30 years |
| **Resignation** | Employee chooses to leave for another opportunity or personal reasons | Employee leaving for a better role at a competitor |
| **Voluntary redundancy** | Employee accepts a redundancy package offered by the employer | NAB offering voluntary redundancy to staff affected by automation |

### 7.2 Involuntary Separation

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Redundancy** | Position is no longer needed due to restructuring, technology, or downsizing | NAB making 12% of staff redundant due to automation |
| **Summary/Instant dismissal** | Immediate termination for serious misconduct | Employee caught stealing or committing fraud |
| **Termination for poor performance** | Dismissal after repeated failure to meet standards despite warnings | Employee consistently failing to meet KPIs after support and training |

### 7.3 Dismissal Process

A fair and legal dismissal process typically follows these steps:

```
Step 1: VERBAL WARNING ──▶ Step 2: WRITTEN WARNING ──▶ Step 3: SECOND WRITTEN WARNING ──▶ Step 4: TERMINATION
```

| Step | Action | Documentation |
|------|--------|---------------|
| **1. Verbal warning** | Manager discusses the issue with the employee; sets expectations for improvement | Note of meeting kept on file |
| **2. Written warning** | Formal letter outlining the issue, expected improvements, and consequences of further poor performance | Letter given to employee; signed and filed |
| **3. Second written warning** | Final warning if issues persist; clearly states next step is termination | Formal document; may involve HR and union representative |
| **4. Termination** | Employment is ended; employee given final pay, notice period, or payment in lieu of notice | Termination letter; final payslip; certificate of service |

**Important legal requirements for dismissal:**
- Must be **genuinely based on valid reasons** (performance, conduct, redundancy)
- Must follow **procedural fairness** (proper process, right to respond)
- Must not be **harsh, unjust, or unreasonable** (unfair dismissal claim possible)
- Minimum **notice periods** must be provided or paid in lieu
- **Redundancy pay** must be provided based on years of service
- Must comply with the **Fair Work Act 2009** and applicable Modern Award

### 7.4 Redundancy — NAB Case Study

> **NAB (National Australia Bank)**: In response to increasing automation and digital banking, NAB reduced its workforce by approximately **12%** — making thousands of roles redundant. However, NAB simultaneously invested in creating **2,000 new technology-focused positions** in areas like data analytics, cybersecurity, and digital customer experience. This demonstrates:
> - **Restructuring** in response to technological change
> - Need for **retraining and redeployment** of existing staff
> - Balance between **cost reduction** and **investment in future capabilities**
> - Importance of **fair redundancy processes** and **outplacement support**

### 7.5 Unfair Dismissal

**Definition**: Dismissal that is **harsh, unjust, or unreasonable** — or not based on a **genuine redundancy** or the employee's **capacity/conduct**.

| Criterion | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Eligibility** | Employee must have completed minimum employment period (6 months for large employers, 12 months for small) |
| **Time limit** | Claim must be lodged within 21 days of dismissal |
| **Fair Work Commission** | Adjudicates unfair dismissal claims |
| **Remedies** | Reinstatement (return to job) or compensation (up to 26 weeks' pay) |
| **Small business exception** | Different rules apply for businesses with <15 employees |

## 8. Motivation Theories

Understanding motivation is essential for HRM — it determines how businesses design rewards, manage performance, and structure work.

### 8.1 Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Abraham Maslow proposed that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy — lower-level needs must be satisfied before higher-level needs become motivating.

```
                          ╱╲
                         ╱  ╲
                        ╱ 5  ╲
                       ╱ SELF ╲
                      ╱ACTUAL- ╲
                     ╱  ISATION  ╲
                    ╱  (reach full ╲
                   ╱   potential)   ╲
                  ╱─────────────────╲
                 ╱      4. ESTEEM    ╲
                ╱   (recognition,    ╲
               ╱    status, respect)  ╲
              ╱───────────────────────╲
             ╱  3. SOCIAL/BELONGING    ╲
            ╱  (teamwork, friendship,  ╲
           ╱      relationships)        ╲
          ╱─────────────────────────────╲
         ╱       2. SAFETY NEEDS        ╲
        ╱  (job security, safe workplace,╲
       ╱        fair conditions, health)  ╲
      ╱───────────────────────────────────╲
     ╱       1. PHYSIOLOGICAL NEEDS       ╲
    ╱    (salary, basic pay, shelter,      ╲
   ╱             food, warmth)              ╲
  ╱─────────────────────────────────────────╲
```

| Level | Need | Workplace Application | Example |
|-------|------|----------------------|---------|
| **1. Physiological** | Basic survival needs | Adequate salary to cover living costs; basic working conditions | McDonald's crew receiving minimum wage to cover rent and food |
| **2. Safety** | Security and protection | Job security; safe working conditions (WHS); workers compensation; stable employment | BYD providing safe factory conditions and injury insurance |
| **3. Social/Belonging** | Connection and belonging | Teamwork; social events; supportive management; workplace culture | Apple's collaborative work environment and team-based projects |
| **4. Esteem** | Recognition and status | Job titles; promotions; awards; recognition programs; praise | NAB's "Employee of the Quarter" recognition program |
| **5. Self-Actualisation** | Personal growth and fulfilment | Challenging work; creative freedom; leadership opportunities; career advancement | Apple encouraging employees to pursue innovative projects and cross-functional roles |

**HRM Application:**
- Pay must be sufficient to meet physiological needs before expecting motivation
- WHS policies and job security address safety needs
- Team-building activities and inclusive culture support social needs
- Recognition programs and promotion pathways address esteem needs
- Training, development, and challenging work support self-actualisation

> **Exam tip:** When applying Maslow in an essay, always work from the bottom up — explain how lower needs must be met first, then show how higher-level rewards can motivate.

### 8.2 Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Frederick Herzberg identified two categories of factors that influence job satisfaction and motivation:

| Factor Type | Name | Effect When Absent | Effect When Present | Examples |
|-------------|------|-------------------|--------------------| ---------|
| **Hygiene Factors** | Dissatisfaction-prevention factors | Cause dissatisfaction | Do NOT motivate — only prevent dissatisfaction | Salary, working conditions, job security, company policy, supervision, interpersonal relationships |
| **Motivators** | Satisfaction/motivation factors | No satisfaction | Actually MOTIVATE and satisfy employees | Achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, personal growth, meaningful work |

| Factor | Hygiene (Dissatisfaction Preventers) | Motivators (Satisfaction Creators) |
|--------|--------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Nature** | Extrinsic to the work itself | Intrinsic to the work itself |
| **Effect** | Eliminate dissatisfaction but don't create motivation | Create genuine satisfaction and motivation |
| **Example** | Paying a fair salary (prevents complaints) | Giving responsibility for an important project (creates engagement) |
| **Analogy** | Like the basics of a house — roof, plumbing (prevent problems) | Like decorating the house — makes it enjoyable |

**HRM Application:**
- First, ensure hygiene factors are adequate (fair pay, safe conditions, good policies)
- Then, design roles with motivators (recognition, growth, responsibility, autonomy)
- Simply increasing pay (hygiene) won't sustain motivation — must combine with motivators

> **Apple**: Apple addresses hygiene factors (competitive salaries, safe workplaces) but focuses heavily on motivators — giving employees meaningful work, creative autonomy, recognition for innovation, and opportunities for personal growth.

### 8.3 McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

Douglas McGregor proposed two contrasting management assumptions about employee motivation:

| Dimension | Theory X (Authoritarian) | Theory Y (Participative) |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Assumption about workers** | Workers are inherently lazy, dislike work, and avoid responsibility | Workers are self-motivated, enjoy work, and seek responsibility |
| **Management approach** | Command and control; close supervision; strict rules | Empowerment; trust; delegation; collaborative |
| **Motivation method** | Extrinsic — punishment and rewards ("carrot and stick") | Intrinsic — meaningful work, autonomy, achievement |
| **Communication** | Top-down; one-way | Two-way; open; participative |
| **Decision-making** | Centralised — managers decide | Decentralised — employees involved |
| **Control** | Tight monitoring and surveillance | Outcome-based; trust employees |
| **Example workplace** | Traditional factory assembly line; call centre with scripts | Apple's innovation labs; BYD's R&D teams |
| **Suitability** | Routine, repetitive tasks where output is easily measured | Creative, knowledge-based work requiring innovation |

| Application | Theory X | Theory Y |
|-------------|----------|----------|
| **Recruitment** | Focus on compliance and reliability | Focus on creativity and self-direction |
| **Training** | Task-specific, procedural | Broad development, problem-solving |
| **Performance management** | Output-based KPIs, strict monitoring | Goal-based, autonomous, feedback-oriented |
| **Remuneration** | Piece rate, bonuses for output | Profit sharing, intrinsic rewards, growth opportunities |
| **Structure** | Tall, centralised, functional | Flat, decentralised, matrix |

> **Exam tip:** Most modern businesses use a **combination** of Theory X and Theory Y depending on the role. Factory workers may need more Theory X management, while Apple's designers thrive under Theory Y conditions.

## 9. Organisational Structures

The way a business is structured directly affects HRM — it determines reporting lines, communication flows, decision-making authority, and career pathways.

### 9.1 Flat vs Tall Structures

| Feature | Flat Structure | Tall Structure |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| **Management layers** | Few (2-3 levels) | Many (5+ levels) |
| **Span of control** | Wide — managers oversee many employees | Narrow — managers oversee few employees |
| **Communication** | Fast — fewer levels to pass through | Slow — information filtered through many layers |
| **Decision-making** | Faster — less bureaucracy | Slower — more approvals needed |
| **Employee autonomy** | Higher — more responsibility | Lower — closer supervision |
| **Career paths** | Fewer promotion opportunities | Clear career ladder |
| **Cost** | Lower overhead (fewer managers) | Higher overhead (more management salaries) |
| **Best suited to** | Small businesses, startups, creative firms | Large corporations, government, regulated industries |
| **Example** | Apple's design team (collaborative, few layers) | McDonald's corporate hierarchy (standardised, many levels) |

### 9.2 Centralised vs Decentralised Structures

| Feature | Centralised | Decentralised |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| **Decision-making** | At the top — senior management | Pushed down to lower levels |
| **Consistency** | High — uniform decisions across the organisation | May vary between departments/locations |
| **Speed** | Slower for local decisions | Faster — local managers can respond quickly |
| **Employee empowerment** | Low — limited autonomy | High — employees have ownership |
| **Accountability** | Clear — decisions traceable to top | Distributed — harder to trace |
| **Best suited to** | Small businesses, crisis situations, standardised operations | Large businesses, diverse markets, innovation-driven |
| **Example** | McDonald's (standardised global operations) | BYD (decentralised innovation across EV, battery, and tech divisions) |

### 9.3 Functional vs Matrix Structures

| Feature | Functional Structure | Matrix Structure |
|---------|---------------------|------------------|
| **Organisation** | Grouped by function (marketing, finance, operations, HR) | Dual reporting — by function AND project/product |
| **Specialisation** | High — experts grouped together | Moderate — cross-functional teams |
| **Efficiency** | High within functions | High across functions |
| **Communication** | Within function is good; across functions is poor ("silos") | Encourages cross-functional communication |
| **Flexibility** | Low — rigid departmental boundaries | High — teams formed for specific projects |
| **Complexity** | Simple — clear reporting lines | Complex — dual reporting creates ambiguity |
| **Best suited to** | Stable environments; single product/service | Complex, dynamic environments; multiple projects |
| **Example** | McDonald's (separate departments for operations, marketing, finance) | Apple (cross-functional teams for product development) |

### 9.4 Other Organisational Structures

| Structure | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Divisional** | Organised by product, geography, or market | Telstra (divided by consumer, business, enterprise) |
| **Network** | Core functions in-house; non-core outsourced | Nike (design in-house; manufacturing outsourced) |
| **Team-based** | Organised around self-managed teams | Google (cross-functional project teams) |
| **Flatarch** | Hybrid of flat and hierarchy — flat for innovation, hierarchy for operations | Amazon (innovation teams operate flatly within a tall corporate structure) |

### 9.5 Organisational Structure and HRM

| Structure | Impact on HRM |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Flat** | Less hierarchical promotion paths; more emphasis on skills and performance; wider spans of management |
| **Tall** | Clear career progression; more management roles to fill; formal training for each level |
| **Centralised** | HR policies applied uniformly; less local flexibility; efficient for compliance |
| **Decentralised** | HR decisions made locally; more autonomy for managers; harder to ensure consistency |
| **Functional** | Specialist HR roles; deep expertise in HR functions |
| **Matrix** | HR must manage complex reporting lines; dual performance appraisals; conflict resolution skills needed |

## 10. Workplace Diversity and Flexible Work Practices

### 10.1 Workplace Diversity

**Definition**: Workplace diversity means having a workforce composed of people with different backgrounds, characteristics, and perspectives — including gender, age, ethnicity, cultural background, disability, sexual orientation, religion, and experience.

**Benefits of workplace diversity:**

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Wider talent pool** | Recruiting from all demographics increases the range of available skills and experience |
| **Better decision-making** | Diverse perspectives lead to more creative solutions and better problem-solving |
| **Improved creativity** | Different backgrounds bring different approaches and ideas |
| **Better understanding of customers** | A diverse workforce can relate to a diverse customer base |
| **Enhanced employer reputation** | Known as an inclusive employer attracts top talent |
| **Legal compliance** | Meets anti-discrimination and EEO legal requirements |
| **Reduced risk of discrimination claims** | Proactive diversity reduces litigation risk |

**Challenges of workplace diversity:**

| Challenge | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Communication barriers** | Language differences, cultural norms can cause misunderstandings |
| **Potential for discrimination/bias** | Unconscious bias may affect recruitment, promotion, and daily interactions |
| **Different work styles** | Cultural differences in approach to authority, teamwork, and communication |
| **Training costs** | Diversity and inclusion training requires investment |
| **Resistance to change** | Some employees may resist diversity initiatives |
| **Conflict management** | Different perspectives can lead to disagreements if not managed well |

**Diversity strategies:**

| Strategy | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Affirmative action** | Active recruitment and promotion of underrepresented groups |
| **Diversity training** | Education on unconscious bias, cultural sensitivity, inclusion |
| **Mentoring programs** | Pairing diverse employees with senior leaders for career development |
| **Flexible work policies** | Accommodating different needs (religious observances, disability, family) |
| **Diversity targets** | Setting measurable goals for representation at all levels |
| **Employee Resource Groups (ERGs)** | Self-formed groups supporting specific communities (women, LGBTQ+, Indigenous) |

> **BYD**: BYD operates across multiple countries and cultures, requiring HR to manage a highly diverse workforce. Their approach includes cultural sensitivity training and inclusive hiring practices across their global operations.

### 10.2 Flexible Work Practices

**Definition**: Flexible work practices are arrangements that give employees greater control over when, where, and how they work — moving beyond the traditional 9-to-5, on-site model.

| Practice | Description | Pros | Cons |
|----------|-------------|------|------|
| **Part-time** | Fewer hours than full-time (e.g., 20 hrs/week) | Work-life balance; attracts parents, students | May limit career advancement; pro-rata entitlements |
| **Job sharing** | Two employees share one full-time role | Both get flexibility; employer gets 2 perspectives | Coordination challenges; harder to manage |
| **Telecommuting/WFH** | Working from home or remote location | No commute; reduced office costs; broader talent pool | Isolation; harder to supervise; blurs work-life boundary |
| **Flextime** | Flexible start/finish times within core hours | Employees choose optimal hours; reduced peak-hour travel | Coordination difficulties; may not suit all roles |
| **Compressed work week** | Full hours in fewer days (e.g., 4 × 10-hour days) | Long weekends; reduced commute days | Longer daily hours may cause fatigue |
| **Annualised hours** | Total annual hours agreed, worked flexibly across the year | Matches seasonal demand; employee control | Complex to manage and track |
| **Shift swapping** | Employees trade shifts with colleagues | Employee autonomy; covers absences | May create skill gaps; management overhead |

**Benefits of flexible work for the business:**
- Improved work-life balance → higher morale and retention
- Access to wider talent pool (parents, people with disabilities, regional candidates)
- Reduced absenteeism — employees manage their own schedules
- Lower office costs — less desk space needed
- Increased productivity — employees work during their most productive hours
- Competitive advantage in talent attraction

**Challenges of flexible work for the business:**
- Coordination difficulties — harder to schedule meetings and collaborative work
- Reduced team cohesion and social interaction
- Harder to monitor performance and productivity
- Not suitable for all roles (manufacturing, retail, healthcare)
- Technology requirements for remote work (devices, software, cybersecurity)
- Potential for overwork — blurred boundaries between work and personal life

> **Apple**: Apple initially required employees to return to the office post-COVID, reflecting its emphasis on in-person collaboration. However, it eventually adopted a hybrid model for many roles, recognising the need for flexibility while preserving its culture of innovation.

> **McDonald's**: McDonald's offers limited flexible work for corporate employees but faces constraints in restaurants where physical presence is essential. This illustrates how flexibility is role-dependent — not all positions can be adapted.

## 11. HSC Exam Response Structures

### 11.1 Command Term Marks Guide

| Verb | Marks Available | What You Must Do | Example Response Opening |
|------|----------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Identify** | 1-2 | Name or state a concept, term, or factor | "Recruitment is the process of attracting and selecting suitable candidates for a position." |

| **Explain** | 3-4 | How or why something happens, with an example | "Businesses use internal recruitment because it is cheaper and faster than external recruitment, as existing employees are already known to the organisation." |
| **Analyse** | 4-5 | Break down into parts, examine relationships between factors | "Internal recruitment motivates existing employees by showing clear career pathways, which can reduce turnover. However, it limits the talent pool, potentially missing out on fresh perspectives." |

However, the process is costly and time-consuming, which may deter some candidates." |

### 11.2 PEEL Structure (Paragraph Writing)

Use PEEL for **every body paragraph** in extended responses:

| Letter | Meaning | What to Write | Example |
|--------|---------|---------------|---------|
| **P** | Point | State the main argument/topic sentence | "One benefit of on-the-job training is that it is immediately relevant to the employee's role." |
| **E** | Evidence/Example | Provide specific evidence, data, or a case study | "For example, McDonald's uses on-the-job training where experienced crew members coach new employees on standardised food preparation procedures." |
| **E** | Explanation | Explain HOW the evidence supports the point | "This means new employees learn practical skills in the actual work environment, reducing the time to full productivity and ensuring consistency with McDonald's quality standards." |
| **L** | Link | Connect back to the question and/or business | "Therefore, on-the-job training is an effective strategy for McDonald's because it maintains service quality while minimising training costs — directly supporting its cost leadership strategy." |

### 11.3 HR Extended Response Template (8-10 marks)

**Introduction (2-3 sentences):**
- Define the key HR concept from the question
- Briefly outline what the response will discuss
- Mention the business/case study being analysed

**Body Paragraph 1 (PEEL):**
- First advantage/strategy/argument
- Evidence from case study
- Explanation of how it benefits the business
- Link back to question

**Body Paragraph 2 (PEEL):**
- Second advantage/strategy/argument
- Evidence from case study
- Explanation with reference to theory (Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor)
- Link back to question

**Body Paragraph 3 (PEEL):**
- Counter-argument or limitation
- Evidence showing the downside
- Explanation of when/why this occurs
- Link — why the advantages still outweigh

**Conclusion (2-3 sentences):**
- Summarise the key arguments
- Make a clear judgement/recommendation
- Link to overall business performance

### 11.4 Recommendation Response Template (6-8 marks)

Use this structure for "recommend" questions:

| Step | Action | Example |
|------|--------|---------|
| **1. Recommend** | State the recommendation clearly | "It is recommended that NAB implement a comprehensive retraining program for employees affected by automation." |
| **2. Define** | Define the recommendation | "A retraining program involves systematically upskilling employees whose roles are being automated, equipping them with new capabilities for technology-focused positions." |
| **3. Reason 1** | Explain how it addresses the identified problem | "This would address the issue of redundancy by redeploying existing staff into the 2,000 new technology roles NAB is creating, reducing recruitment costs and retaining institutional knowledge." |
| **4. Reason 2** | Provide an additional supporting argument | "Additionally, retraining demonstrates NAB's commitment to its employees, improving morale and reducing the risk of negative publicity associated with mass redundancies." |
| **5. Link** | Restate the recommendation with justification | "Therefore, implementing a retraining program would enable NAB to transition its workforce efficiently while maintaining employee engagement and operational capability." |

### 11.5 Applying Motivation Theories in Exam Responses

| Theory | How to Apply in an Essay |
|--------|-------------------------|
| **Maslow** | Identify which level of needs the scenario addresses. Show how lower needs must be met first, then explain how higher-level rewards (recognition, growth) can further motivate. |
| **Herzberg** | Distinguish between hygiene factors (prevent dissatisfaction) and motivators (create motivation). Show that pay increases alone won't sustain motivation — must combine with recognition and growth. |
| **McGregor** | Identify whether the management style is Theory X or Theory Y. Explain which is more appropriate for the situation. Argue that modern businesses increasingly use Theory Y for knowledge workers. |

**Example paragraph applying Maslow:**
> "NAB's decision to offer retraining rather than outright redundancy addresses employees' safety needs (Maslow Level 2) by providing job security during a period of significant organisational change. Furthermore, by offering training in new technology skills, NAB supports employees' self-actualisation needs (Level 5), enabling them to reach their full potential in emerging roles. This combination of addressing lower and higher-level needs is likely to maintain employee motivation and loyalty during the transition."

## 12. Case Studies

### 12.1 Apple — Innovation Through People

| HR Area | Apple's Approach |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Recruitment** | 2–7 interviews per candidate; looks for cross-collaboration, flexibility, adaptability, problem-solving |
| **Training** | Extensive on-the-job training; internal mobility between teams; leadership development programs |
| **Motivation** | Theory Y management; creative autonomy; meaningful work; recognition of innovation |
| **Structure** | Functional with matrix elements — cross-functional teams for product development |
| **Rewards** | Competitive salaries; ESPP (Employee Stock Purchase Plan); comprehensive benefits; intrinsic rewards from meaningful work |
| **Culture** | Innovation-driven; collaboration across functions; secrecy and focus; high performance expectations |
| **Diversity** | Active programs for gender and ethnic diversity; accessibility initiatives |

> **Key exam point:** Apple's HR strategy demonstrates interdependence — its recruitment and training practices directly support its innovation (operations) and brand perception (marketing), while competitive rewards require significant financial investment (finance).

### 12.2 McDonald's — Standardisation and Efficiency

| HR Area | McDonald's Approach |
|---------|---------------------|
| **Recruitment** | High-volume hiring for crew; structured interviews; focuses on reliability and teamwork |
| **Training** | Hamburger University (off-the-job); on-the-job training for food preparation and customer service; standardised procedures |
| **Motivation** | Theory X for routine tasks (standardised procedures); recognition programs; promotional pathways for high performers |
| **Structure** | Tall, centralised — standardised operations globally; franchise model adds complexity |
| **Rewards** | Hourly wages (above minimum for some roles); crew bonuses; career progression programs |
| **Flexibility** | Limited for restaurant staff (physical presence essential); some for corporate employees |
| **Outsourcing** | Franchise model — franchisees manage local HR; head office sets standards |

> **Key exam point:** McDonald's demonstrates how HRM supports cost leadership — standardised training reduces errors, high-volume recruitment ensures staffing levels, and Theory X management maintains consistency in routine operations.

### 12.3 BYD — Global Growth and Innovation

| HR Area | BYD's Approach |
|---------|----------------|
| **Recruitment** | Massive scale (600,000+ employees); active recruitment of engineers and tech talent; university partnerships |
| **Training** | Technical training for manufacturing; leadership development for managers; cross-functional rotation |
| **Motivation** | Theory Y for R&D teams; innovation incentives; career growth in expanding industry |
| **Structure** | Divisional — separate divisions for EVs, batteries, electronics; matrix elements for cross-division projects |
| **Rewards** | Competitive salaries for tech talent; share options; non-monetary rewards (innovation time) |
| **Diversity** | Global workforce across China, Brazil, Hungary, etc.; cultural sensitivity training |
| **Sustainability** | HR supports BYD's green mission through volunteer programs and ethical employment practices |

> **Key exam point:** BYD's rapid growth (from battery maker to world's largest EV manufacturer) demonstrates how strategic HRM supports business expansion — recruiting for scale, training for capability, and structuring for innovation.

### 12.4 NAB — Restructuring and Digital Transformation

| HR Area | NAB's Approach |
|---------|----------------|
| **Restructuring** | 12% workforce reduction due to automation; redeployment programs for affected staff |
| **Technology** | 2,000 new tech roles created (data, cybersecurity, digital); investment in retraining |
| **Recruitment** | Focus on digital skills; partnerships with universities and tech bootcamps |
| **Training** | Digital upskilling programs; leadership development for new ways of working |
| **Motivation** | Retention strategies during change; transparent communication; outplacement support for redundant staff |

| **Culture** | Transitioning from traditional banking culture to innovation-focused; change management critical |

> **Key exam point:** NAB's experience demonstrates the interdependence of HR with finance (cost reduction vs investment in people), operations (automating processes while creating new roles), and stakeholder management (balancing shareholder demands with employee welfare).

## 13. Key Definitions Quick Reference

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Human Resource Management (HRM)** | The strategic approach to managing an organisation's people — recruiting, developing, rewarding, and retaining employees |
| **Strategic Role of HRM** | HR's proactive, long-term contribution to business planning and goal achievement |
| **Interdependence** | The mutual reliance between HR and other business functions (operations, marketing, finance) |
| **HR Cycle** | The four stages of managing employees: Acquisition → Development → Maintenance → Separation |
| **Acquisition** | The process of identifying, recruiting, selecting, and inducting new employees |
| **Development** | Building employee skills through training, performance management, and career planning |
| **Maintenance** | Keeping employees motivated and committed through monetary, non-monetary, and intrinsic rewards |
| **Separation** | When an employee leaves the organisation (voluntarily or involuntarily) |
| **Induction** | The process of introducing a new employee to the organisation, their role, and workplace expectations |
| **On-the-Job Training** | Learning while performing the actual work (mentoring, coaching, job rotation, shadowing) |
| **Off-the-Job Training** | Learning away from the workplace (conferences, TAFE, online courses, workshops) |
| **KPIs (Key Performance Indicators)** | Measurable targets used to evaluate employee or business performance |
| **360-Degree Feedback** | Performance evaluation using input from supervisors, peers, subordinates, and self-assessment |
| **Balanced Scorecard** | Performance framework measuring financial, customer, internal process, and learning/growth perspectives |
| **Performance Appraisal** | A formal, systematic evaluation of an employee's job performance and contribution |
| **Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs** | Theory that human needs are arranged in 5 levels: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation |
| **Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory** | Theory distinguishing hygiene factors (prevent dissatisfaction) from motivators (create satisfaction) |
| **McGregor's Theory X** | Assumption that workers are lazy, avoid work, and need close supervision and control |
| **McGregor's Theory Y** | Assumption that workers are self-motivated, enjoy work, and seek responsibility |
| **Flat Structure** | Organisational structure with few management layers and wide spans of control |
| **Tall Structure** | Organisational structure with many management layers and narrow spans of control |
| **Centralised Structure** | Decision-making authority concentrated at the top of the organisation |
| **Decentralised Structure** | Decision-making authority pushed down to lower levels of the organisation |
| **NES (National Employment Standards)** | 11 minimum employment entitlements under the Fair Work Act 2009 |
| **Fair Work Act 2009** | National legislation governing workplace relations, awards, enterprise agreements, and unfair dismissal |
| **WHS Act 2011** | Work Health and Safety legislation requiring employer duty of care for employee safety |
| **Anti-Discrimination Acts** | Legislation prohibiting discrimination based on race, gender, age, disability, and other protected attributes |
| **EEO (Equal Employment Opportunity)** | Ensuring fair and non-discriminatory practices in recruitment, promotion, and treatment |
| **Enterprise Agreement** | Negotiated agreement between employer and employees (or union) covering pay and conditions |
| **Modern Award** | Industry-specific document setting minimum pay rates and conditions |
| **BOOT Test** | Better Off Overall Test — enterprise agreements must leave employees better off than the relevant Award |
| **Casual Loading** | Higher hourly rate paid to casual employees to compensate for lack of leave entitlements |
| **Workers Compensation** | Mandatory insurance covering employees for work-related injuries and illnesses |
| **Redundancy** | Termination of employment because the position is no longer needed |
| **Unfair Dismissal** | Dismissal that is harsh, unjust, or unreasonable — claimable through Fair Work Commission |
| **Outsourcing** | Contracting external providers to perform specific business functions |
| **Contractors** | Self-employed individuals who provide services under contract — not employees |
| **Domestic Contractors** | Australian-based contractors providing specialist services |
| **Global Contractors** | Offshore contractors providing services at lower cost |
| **Monetary Rewards** | Financial compensation including salary, wages, commission, bonuses, profit sharing, fringe benefits |
| **Non-Monetary Rewards** | Rewards without direct financial value — job variety, flexible hours, status, autonomy |
| **Intrinsic Rewards** | Internal psychological rewards from the work itself — achievement, purpose, mastery |
| **Remuneration Package** | Comprehensive combination of monetary and non-monetary rewards |
| **Fringe Benefits** | Non-cash benefits with monetary value — company car, health insurance, phone |
| **Piece Rate** | Payment per unit produced rather than per hour worked |
| **Commission** | Payment based on a percentage of sales generated |
| **CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility)** | Business practices that benefit society — ethical employment, volunteering, community engagement |
| **Affirmative Action** | Active recruitment and promotion of underrepresented groups to address inequality |
| **Multiskilling** | Training employees in multiple skills to increase flexibility and reduce reliance on specialists |
| **Restructuring** | Reorganising the business structure, often involving redundancies and new roles |
| **Productivity** | Output per unit of input — higher productivity means more efficient use of resources |
| **Feminisation of the Workforce** | The increasing participation of women in the workforce |
| **Code of Conduct** | Document outlining expected workplace behaviour and ethical standards |
| **Duty of Care** | Legal obligation of employers to provide a safe workplace (physical and psychological) |
| **Employment Contract** | Legal agreement between employer and employee outlining terms and conditions |
| **Minimum Wage** | The lowest legal hourly rate an employer can pay ($26.44/hr as of 2024) |
| **Enterprise Bargaining** | The process of negotiating an enterprise agreement between employer and employees/union |
| **Industrial Action** | Actions taken by employees (strike) or employer (lockout) during bargaining disputes |
| **Strike** | Employees refusing to work as a form of industrial action |
| **Lockout** | Employer preventing employees from working as a form of industrial action |
| **Workplace Diversity** | A workforce composed of people with different backgrounds, characteristics, and perspectives |
| **Flexible Work Practices** | Arrangements giving employees control over when, where, and how they work |
| **Job Sharing** | Two employees sharing one full-time role |
| **Telecommuting** | Working from home or a remote location using technology |
| **Flextime** | Flexible start and finish times within agreed core hours |
| **Compressed Work Week** | Working full-time hours in fewer days (e.g., 4 × 10-hour days) |

## 14. Exam Tips and Common Mistakes

### 14.1 Top 10 HR Exam Tips

1. **Always demonstrate interdependence** — show how HR decisions affect operations, marketing, finance, and stakeholders
2. **Use specific case study evidence** — Apple, McDonald's, BYD, NAB are all mentioned throughout these notes
3. **Apply motivation theories** — don't just define Maslow/Herzberg/McGregor, apply them to the scenario
4. **Distinguish between hygiene factors and motivators** — a common exam trap
5. **Know the NES** — be able to list at least 5 of the 11 National Employment Standards
6. **Use the PEEL structure** for every body paragraph
7. **Address both advantages AND disadvantages** — discuss questions require multiple perspectives
8. **Link back to the question** in every paragraph — don't drift off-topic
9. **Define key terms** at the start of your response — this earns easy marks
10. **Make a clear judgement** in evaluate questions — don't sit on the fence

### 14.2 Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | How to Fix It |
|---------|---------------|
| Defining but not explaining | Always explain HOW and WHY, not just WHAT |
| Ignoring the question focus | Re-read the question after every paragraph — are you answering it? |
| Using generic examples | Use specific case study evidence (Apple, McDonald's, BYD, NAB) |
| Forgetting to evaluate | For "discuss" and "evaluate" questions, present BOTH sides |
| Not using HR terminology | Use terms like "induction," "KPIs," "enterprise agreement," "NES" |
| Confusing Theory X and Theory Y | Theory X = authoritarian (workers lazy); Theory Y = participative (workers motivated) |
| Confusing hygiene factors and motivators | Hygiene = prevent dissatisfaction (pay, conditions); Motivators = create satisfaction (recognition, growth) |
| Ignoring the legal framework | Reference Fair Work Act, NES, WHS Act, anti-discrimination when relevant |
| Writing too much / too little | Match your response length to the marks available |
| Not structuring your response | Use PEEL paragraphs and clear intro/body/conclusion |

### 14.3 Quick Response Planning Guide

| Question Type | Marks | Time | Structure |
|---------------|-------|------|-----------|
| Short answer (identify/describe) | 1-3 | 2-4 min | Direct answer; define term; brief explanation |
| Short answer (explain) | 3-5 | 5-8 min | Define; explain how/why; example; link |
| Extended response (analyse/discuss) | 6-8 | 10-15 min | Intro; 2-3 PEEL paragraphs (for + against); conclusion |
| Extended response (evaluate/recommend) | 8-10 | 15-20 min | Intro; 2-3 PEEL paragraphs; clear judgement; recommendation |
