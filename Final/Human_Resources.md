## 1. Role of Human Resource Management

**Definition**: Human resource management is the **management of people** to achieve business objectives. It is one of the four key business functions.

### 1.1 Strategic Role of Human Resources

- HRM aligns the **workforce** with the **strategic direction** of the business
- Attracts, develops and **retains** talented employees
- Creates a positive **corporate culture**
- Manages employee relations and legal compliance
- Employees are treated as an **asset to be developed**, not a cost to be minimised

> **Link to performance**: Skilled, motivated employees → higher productivity → better operations, more effective marketing, and lower costs to finance. This is the argument to make in every extended response.

### 1.2 Interdependence with Other Key Business Functions

| Function | Relationship with HR |
|---|---|
| **Operations** | HR recruits and trains production staff. Operations determines skill needs and staffing levels. Productivity depends on workforce quality |
| **Marketing** | HR recruits the marketing team. Marketing effectiveness depends on staff creativity and skills. Customer-facing staff need service training |
| **Finance** | Wages and training are major budget items. Finance sets salary budgets; HR must justify expenditure through productivity gains |

> **Exam tip**: Always show how HR connects to the other three functions — **interdependence** is a guaranteed exam concept.

### 1.3 Outsourcing HR Functions

Businesses increasingly outsource HR activities to external providers.

| Type | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Human resource functions** | Recruitment, payroll, training, and specialist advice contracted to external firms |
| **Using contractors** | Engaging workers who are **not employees**. Provides flexibility and avoids on-costs |
| **Domestic contractors** | Located within Australia |
| **Global contractors** | Located overseas — lower cost, but risks in quality, communication, time zones and legal jurisdiction |

> **Confusing pair — Employee vs Contractor**
> An **employee** works under a contract *of* service — the business controls how, when and where they work, and owes them leave, superannuation and award conditions.
> A **contractor** works under a contract *for* service — they control their own methods, invoice for a result, and are responsible for their own tax and insurance.
> Misclassifying an employee as a contractor ("**sham contracting**") is illegal.

### 1.4 Stakeholders in Human Resources

| Stakeholder | Interest |
|---|---|
| **Employers** | Productivity, profitability, flexibility, retaining skilled staff |
| **Employees** | Fair pay, safe conditions, job security, career development, work–life balance |
| **Employer associations** | Represent and advise employers in negotiations and disputes |
| **Unions** | Represent employees; negotiate pay and conditions; protect rights |
| **Government organisations** | Fair Work Commission, Fair Work Ombudsman, WHS regulators, Australian Human Rights Commission — set and enforce the legal framework |
| **Society** | Expects ethical treatment, safe workplaces, diversity, and fair employment practices |

> Stakeholders often have **conflicting** interests: employers want cost flexibility, employees want security. Good HRM manages that tension.

## 2. Influences on Human Resources

| Influence | Impact |
|---|---|
| **Stakeholders** | Competing demands from employers, employees, unions, government and society shape HR policy |
| **Legal** | Fair Work Act 2009, WHS Act, anti-discrimination law, awards, the NES — set minimum standards |
| **Economic** | In a **boom**: low unemployment, skills shortages, wage pressure, harder to recruit. In a **downturn**: higher unemployment, easier recruitment, pressure for redundancies |
| **Technological** | Remote work, automation of HR processes (HRIS), AI recruitment, video interviews. Changes the **skills** required and can displace roles |
| **Social** | Changing work patterns, an ageing workforce, increased workforce participation of women, greater cultural diversity, demand for work–life balance |
| **Ethics and CSR** | Fair pay, diversity and inclusion, bullying and harassment prevention, whistleblower protection, safe working conditions throughout the supply chain |

### 2.1 The Legal Framework

#### The Employment Contract

The **employment contract** is a legally binding agreement between employer and employee. It may be created through:

| Instrument | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Common law contract** | The individual agreement between employer and employee. Both parties have common law **obligations** — the employer must pay wages and provide a safe workplace; the employee must obey lawful instructions and act in good faith |
| **Awards** | Industry- or occupation-specific **minimum** pay and conditions set by the Fair Work Commission. A **safety net** |
| **Enterprise agreements** | Collective agreements negotiated at the workplace between employer and employees (often with a union). Must pass the **Better Off Overall Test (BOOT)** — employees must be better off than under the relevant award |
| **National Employment Standards (NES)** | The legislated minimum entitlements that apply to all employees regardless of any award or agreement |

> **Confusing pair — Award vs Enterprise Agreement**
> An **award** is an industry-wide **safety net** set by the Fair Work Commission — it applies by default.
> An **enterprise agreement** is negotiated **at a specific workplace** and must leave employees **better off overall** than the award. Awards are the floor; agreements build above it.

#### Key Legislation

| Law | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Fair Work Act 2009** | The national workplace relations system — minimum wages, the NES, unfair dismissal, enterprise bargaining, the Fair Work Commission |
| **Work Health and Safety Act** | Employer **duty of care**; hazard identification, risk assessment and control; consultation with workers |
| **Anti-discrimination legislation** | Prohibits discrimination on grounds including sex, race, age, disability, marital status, religion, sexual orientation |
| **Equal Employment Opportunity (EEO)** | Requires that employment decisions are based on **merit** and that barriers for disadvantaged groups are removed |
| **Employment Protection Act 1982 (NSW)** | Provides a **minimum scale for severance payments** for permanent employees under NSW awards, for employers with more than 15 employees |

#### Work Health and Safety and Workers Compensation

- **WHS**: The employer has a **duty of care** to provide a safe workplace — safe equipment, training, supervision, and consultation.
- **Workers compensation**: A no-fault insurance scheme providing income and medical costs to workers injured at work. Employers must hold a policy.
- **Cost of failure**: injury, absenteeism, workers compensation premiums, legal penalties, damaged reputation, low morale.

#### Termination and Unfair Dismissal

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Unfair dismissal** | Dismissal that is **harsh, unjust or unreasonable** — claimable through the **Fair Work Commission** |
| **Unlawful termination** | Dismissal for a **prohibited reason** — e.g. pregnancy, race, union membership, temporary absence due to illness |
| **Constructive dismissal** | The employee resigns because the employer's conduct made continued employment intolerable |
| **Summary dismissal** | Immediate dismissal for **serious misconduct** (theft, violence, fraud) |

**Worked process — dismissing for poor performance without triggering an unfair dismissal claim**

An employer cannot dismiss instantly for poor performance. A documented process must be followed, and it must be **written into the employment contract**:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1. Verbal warning** | Recorded on the staff file. The employer **must offer assistance and training**; all support given must be documented |
| **2. First written warning** | If no improvement in the given time despite assistance, a written warning is filed. A set time to improve is given, with further support documented |
| **3. Second written warning** | If still no improvement, a further written warning is issued with a final set time to improve |
| **4. Termination** | If the employee has still not improved, employment can be terminated |

This process **takes a long time and requires accurate documentation**, in case the employee sues for unfair dismissal. Failing to follow it exposes the business to a claim.

## 3. Processes of Human Resource Management — The HR Cycle

> **The HR cycle is the spine of this topic.** Four stages, in order:

```
   ACQUISITION  →  DEVELOPMENT  →  MAINTENANCE  →  SEPARATION
   (get them)      (grow them)     (keep them)     (exit them)
        ↑                                               │
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     (cycle repeats)
```

### 3.1 Acquisition

The stage that involves **identifying staff needs, recruitment and selection**.

**Identifying needs**: If there is a missing skill, or more work than current workers can do in a timely manner, the business may need to recruit.

Once the need is established, the business must write:

| Document | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Job description** | Describes the **tasks and responsibilities** of the employee |
| **Job specification** | Defines the **skills, knowledge and abilities** required for the job |

> **Confusing pair — Job description vs Job specification**
> **Description** describes the **JOB** (what the role does).
> **Specification** specifies the **PERSON** (what they need to have).
> Mnemonic: de**S**cription = the po**S**ition; **spec**ification = the **spec**ial qualities of the person.

#### Recruitment

**Recruitment** = where the business accumulates a **pool of potential candidates** for a job.

| Method | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Internal** | Moving or promoting existing people |
| **External** | Outsourcing to private employment agencies; interviews on university campuses; trainee positions through **cadetships** |

| | **Internal** | **External** |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Cheaper, faster, motivates staff, known performance, shorter induction | Fresh ideas, larger talent pool, new perspectives, brings in scarce skills |
| **Disadvantages** | Limited pool, creates another vacancy, risk of "inbreeding" of ideas | Expensive, unknown performance, longer adjustment and induction |

#### Selection

**Selection** = a **screening process** in staff acquisition. Information gathered about applicants is reviewed and the **most appropriate** applicant is chosen.

Information is gathered through:

- **Interviews** — one-to-one or panel
- **Application forms**
- **Referee checks**
- **Written references**
- **Written / computer tests**

> **Case study — Apple's recruitment**
> *What Apple looks for*: cross-collaboration, flexibility and adaptability to new things, problem solving and thinking outside the box, ability to keep up with a fast pace of work.
> *Apple's selection process*: typically 2–3 interviews and potentially up to 7. Candidates are observed from the start for personality and behaviour; there are introductions and small conversations, candidates are split into sections and placed in scenarios to demonstrate their responses, and asked questions throughout.

#### Induction Training

**Induction** is the **transition from acquisition to development** in the HR cycle. It is the procedure where a new employee is introduced to the business and becomes familiar with how it works.

**What induction must include:**

- Shown all **policies and procedures**
- Introduced to co-workers and **allocated a mentor**
- Understanding of **work health and safety** procedures, including emergency procedures
- Shown where **facilities** are
- **Entitlements** — how breaks work, how long and how often

> **Why induction is necessary for *all* new employees, even experienced ones**: Policies and procedures **differ between businesses**. Even an experienced worker needs to know this business's rules, be introduced to co-workers and a mentor, know what to do in an emergency, know where everything is, and understand their entitlements. It gets employees to a work-ready state faster and saves them the time of finding everything out themselves.

### 3.2 Development

Businesses must develop the skills and capabilities of staff to **maintain competitive advantage**. Development involves preparing employees for **future responsibilities** due to a change in the business's strategies or growth in its size or market share.

> Effective development results in **increased motivation**, **greater business flexibility**, and **improved use of technology and innovation**.

Development includes **training** and **performance appraisals**.

#### Training

**Training** = any activity aimed at improving an employee's **present and future performance** in the workforce.

| Method | Examples |
|---|---|
| **On the job** | Apprenticeships, mentoring, coaching, job rotation, shadowing |
| **Off the job** | External conferences, TAFE courses, university, workshops |

**Benefits of training and development:**

| For the employee | For the business |
|---|---|
| Enhanced skills | More efficient |
| More marketable | More productive |
| Better job satisfaction | |

| For the economy | For the customer |
|---|---|
| More competitive | Gets a better service/product |
| More productive | |

**On the job vs off the job:**

| | **Advantages** | **Disadvantages** |
|---|---|---|
| **On the job** | Generally the most cost effective; employees remain productive; opportunity to learn whilst doing; training alongside real colleagues | Quality depends on the trainer's ability and available time; **bad habits may be passed on**; learning environment may not be conducive; potential disruption to operations |
| **Off the job** | A wider range of skills or qualifications can be obtained; can learn from outside specialists and experts; employees more confident when starting a job | More expensive; lost working time and output; **employees with new qualifications may leave for better jobs** |

#### Performance Appraisal

**Performance appraisal** = a **formal assessment of how well a person is working**. It identifies:

- The person's **strengths**
- Areas that need **further development**
- Areas the employee **would like** further professional development in
- The **professional goals** of the employee

> This forms the basis for **promotions and pay rises**.

**Why it must be managed constructively**: A poorly handled appraisal demotivates, damages trust and increases turnover. A constructive one clarifies expectations, identifies development needs and motivates.

**Why appraisals help employers assess HR effectiveness**: Aggregated appraisal data reveals whether training is working, where skill gaps remain, and whether recruitment is selecting the right people.

**Other performance management methods**: KPIs, **360-degree feedback** (supervisor, peers, subordinates, self), **balanced scorecard** (financial, customer, internal processes, learning and growth).

### 3.3 Maintenance

**Maintenance** is the stage where owners and managers ensure existing staff are:

- Kept **motivated**
- Remain **loyal**
- **Retained**

Staff are maintained by providing working conditions and a work environment that motivate them to be increasingly productive and gain satisfaction from their work.

#### Benefits Used to Maintain Staff

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Monetary benefits** — paid on top of usual entitlements, forming part of the **remuneration package** | Commission; **piece rate** based on output; bonuses for meeting targets; shared ownership schemes; **fringe benefits** (e.g. a company car) |
| **Non-monetary benefits** | Greater job variety; more flexible working hours; increased status in the job or community; **autonomy** in work; access to a subsidised cafeteria |
| **Intrinsic rewards** | A reward that comes **from within the person** — in employment, the feeling of satisfaction that comes with doing a job well |

> **Confusing trio — Monetary / Non-monetary / Intrinsic**
> **Monetary** = money or money's worth, paid by the business.
> **Non-monetary** = a benefit the business provides that isn't cash.
> **Intrinsic** = comes from *inside the person* — the business can create conditions for it but cannot hand it over.

#### Other Maintenance Strategies

| Strategy | Benefit to employer | Benefit to employee |
|---|---|---|
| **Employee participation** | Better decisions, higher commitment, lower turnover | Sense of ownership, voice in decisions, higher satisfaction |
| **Team work** | Improved problem solving, flexibility, peer support | Social belonging, shared load, skill sharing |
| **Flexible working conditions** | Wider talent pool, reduced absenteeism, lower office costs | Work–life balance, reduced stress, retention of carers |
| **Training and rewards** | Higher productivity, skilled workforce, competitive advantage | Career progression, marketability, recognition |

**Flexible work practices**: part-time, **job sharing** (two employees share one full-time role), telecommuting, **flextime** (flexible start/finish with core hours), **compressed working week** (full hours in fewer days).

**Family-friendly programs**: parental leave, carer's leave, on-site childcare, flexible hours. *Likely benefits to the employer*: improved retention of experienced staff, reduced absenteeism, stronger employer brand, wider recruitment pool.

**Workplace diversity**

- *Benefits*: wider talent pool, better decision-making, improved creativity, better understanding of diverse customers, enhanced reputation
- *Challenges*: communication barriers, potential bias, differing work styles, training costs, resistance to change

**The cost of sexual harassment and bullying**: legal liability and compensation, WHS breaches, absenteeism and stress leave, high turnover, low morale and productivity, reputational damage, difficulty recruiting.

### 3.4 Separation

**Separation** is the **ending of the employment relationship**. It may be initiated by either the employee or the employer, and is either **voluntary** or **involuntary**.

| Type | Forms |
|---|---|
| **Voluntary** | **Retirement**; **resignation**; **voluntary redundancy** — where a job is no longer required, or the firm is restructuring or has been taken over |
| **Involuntary** — management decides employees are no longer required | **Involuntary redundancy** — due to firms closing down or new technology (the **Employment Protection Act 1982 (NSW)** provides a minimum scale for severance payments for permanent employees under NSW awards, for employers with more than 15 employees); **instant dismissal** for employees who breach the law; **summary dismissal** for poor work performance |

> **Confusing pair — Redundancy vs Dismissal**
> **Redundancy** = the **JOB** no longer exists (restructuring, technology, closure). It is not about the person's performance.
> **Dismissal** = the **PERSON** is removed (misconduct or poor performance). The job usually still exists.
> This distinction matters legally: redundancy attracts severance pay; dismissal for misconduct generally does not.

**Why involuntary separation must be managed carefully**: risk of unfair dismissal claims, damage to the morale of remaining staff ("survivor syndrome"), reputational damage, loss of corporate knowledge, and legal obligations around notice, consultation and severance.

**Entitlements on redundancy**: notice period (or payment in lieu), redundancy/severance pay based on years of service, accrued annual leave, accrued long service leave where applicable.

**Role of the Fair Work Commission in unfair dismissal**: receives and conciliates claims, determines whether the dismissal was harsh, unjust or unreasonable, and can order **reinstatement** or **compensation**.

> **Case study — NAB**
> NAB announced plans to **downsize its workforce by 12%** to cut operating costs as more processes became automated. The bank planned to shed **6,000 roles** while **adding 2,000 technology jobs** over three years, including specialists in software engineering, data and security. Hiring more tech professionals brings its own challenge because they are **highly sought after**.
>
> *Use this for*: technology as an influence on HR; involuntary redundancy; the changing nature of skills; the tension between cost control (finance) and workforce capability (HR).

### 4.1 Leadership Style

> **Leadership style is the manner and approach in which leaders of a business interact with staff.** Leaders make decisions, develop strategies for the development of the business, and interact with employees.

**Built on three factors:**

```
1. Providing staff with DIRECTION
2. Implementing plans and ORGANISING staff
3. MOTIVATING staff
```

**The eight styles:**

| Style | How it works | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Authoritarian** *(transactional / autocratic)* | Managers are responsible for **telling employees what they want done and how** to accomplish the task | Employees **cannot contribute** to decision-making · decisions made **quickly** |
| **Participative / democratic** | The leader **includes one or more employees** in the decision-making process | Employees consulted on **what** to do and **how**; encouraged to become **empowered**. **The final decision is still the leader's** |
| **Delegative / laissez-faire** | The leader **allows employees to make decisions** | Requires employees with the **relevant knowledge and skills**, and a manager who **trusts** their ability |
| **Pacesetting** | The leader **sets an example** of high performance, high pace and high quality; the team is expected to follow | **Values results above all.** Good for short-term results, but **detrimental to engagement and motivation long term** |
| **Affiliative** | Puts **people first** — creating a harmonious environment and building emotional bonds | Requires **empathy** and the ability to build relationships across communication styles |
| **Coaching** | Characterised by **collaboration, support and guidance** | Focused on **bringing out the best** in the team by guiding them through goals and obstacles |
| **Coercive** | A **command and control** style | Relies on **forcing** people to do what they are told, whether they want to or not |
| **Transformational** | Works with followers **beyond their immediate self-interest** to identify needed change | Creates a **vision**, guides change through **influence and inspiration**, and executes it alongside committed members |

> **Confusing pair — Authoritarian vs Coercive**
> **Authoritarian** = the leader **decides and directs**. Employees don't participate, but they aren't compelled.
> **Coercive** = the leader **forces compliance**, "whether they want it or not."
> Autocratic is about *who decides*. Coercive is about *how compliance is obtained*.

> **Confusing pair — Pacesetting vs Transformational**
> **Pacesetting** models high performance and expects imitation — **values results more than anything**, and burns people out.
> **Transformational** creates a **shared vision** and moves people beyond self-interest.
> Both are demanding. One drives *through* people, the other *with* them.

### 4.2 Job Design

> **Job design is where managers develop and specify the work activities of individuals or groups.** It is the process of determining the types of tasks an employee needs to complete, and of identifying the **skills, knowledge, personal traits, duties and responsibilities** required to perform a position.

**Effective job design seeks to meet the needs of both the business and the employee** — the task must allow the employee to work toward the objectives.

**Job design involves:**

- Creating a **job description**
- **Personal specifications**
- Strategies that improve employee **motivation and productivity**

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Job satisfaction** | The extent to which employees are **satisfied working in their current position** |
| **Job enrichment** | The extent a business **develops tasks with the abilities of existing staff in mind** — reducing the boredom associated with many types of work |

**The six job design strategies:**

```
1. Job rotation                   4. Semi-autonomous work groups
2. Job enlargement                5. Team structures
3. Job enrichment                 6. Flexible work structures
```

> **Confusing trio — Rotation vs Enlargement vs Enrichment**
> **Rotation** — moving **between** different jobs. Variety across time.
> **Enlargement** — **more tasks at the same level**. Horizontal expansion.
> **Enrichment** — **more responsibility and autonomy**. Vertical expansion, matched to ability.
> Rotation changes *where*, enlargement changes *how many*, enrichment changes *how demanding*.

> **General vs specific job design:** **general** design gives employees a variety of tasks, increasing motivation and flexibility. **Specific** design gives a narrow, repetitive set, increasing efficiency and speed but risking boredom and turnover — which is why **specialised job design often reduces job satisfaction**.

### 4.3 Recruitment

> An effective recruitment strategy ensures **the right people are hired to do the right job.**

**The seven consequences of a poor selection process:**

```
1. Increased training costs
2. Increased job dissatisfaction
3. Lower performance
4. Increased labour turnover
5. Increased absenteeism, if the staff member feels inadequate for the job
6. Increased accidents / defect rates
7. Increased claims of discrimination
```

> That list is the answer to *"What is the likely outcome of a poor recruitment decision?"* and to *"How does selecting the wrong employee increase costs and reduce productivity?"*

**Internal vs external:** the choice depends on the **type of skills and qualifications needed**, whether current staff already have them, and whether **recruiting is cheaper than training an existing employee**.

| | General skills | Specific skills |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | Skills, attitudes and behaviours that are a good **cultural fit** | Skills **required for particular jobs** |
| **Includes** | Flexibility and versatility · social confidence · positive attitude · motivation · ability to work in a team **or** independently · leadership and decision-making · willingness to learn · ability to work under pressure | Technical and role-specific capability |
| **Why businesses target them** | Cultural fit and adaptability | **Skill shortages** — filling identified gaps |

### 4.4 Training and Development

> **Crucial to ensuring staff stay motivated, performance goals are met, the possibility of accidents decreases, skill shortages are filled, and staff retention increases.**

**When deciding between developing staff and recruiting, a business considers:**

| Option | |
|---|---|
| Invest further in **in-house training and development** | Retain **experts who retire**, on a part-time basis |
| **Recruit** staff for specific skills | Retain **women through flexible work structures** |
| **Share staff** with other firms | **Outsource** functions to specialist firms |
| **Sponsor overseas migrants** | Build **networks or alliances** with firms holding specialist skills |

> **Confusing pair — Training vs Development**
> **Training** allows employees to perform their **current** roles more effectively.
> **Development** prepares them for **future responsibilities and promotion**.
> Training is about now; development is about next.

### 4.5 Performance Management

> **The process of recognising the efforts and contributions of employees to their work.** It is an **ongoing process of communication** between the direct manager and the employee.

**It involves:**

```
1. Clarification of expectations
2. Setting employee workplace objectives
3. Providing feedback
4. Evaluating employee performance
```

**The three stages of the cycle:**

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Planning** | Expectations and goals developed **by both the employer and the employee** |
| **2. Checking in** | Managers **regularly observe** performance to provide feedback |
| **3. Assessment** | **Measuring performance against the objectives** |

> **Why it is ongoing:** after assessment, **new goals are established, beginning the cycle again.** Performance needs to be continuously monitored, reviewed and improved.

**The four benefits:**

- The employee has an **improved understanding of their role** and what needs to be done
- The employer can **identify problems earlier**
- Reviews make the employee **aware of their personal goals**
- **Identifies areas of development** either needed or requested by the employee

#### ⚠️ Administrative vs Developmental — use your teacher's definition

| | **Administrative** | **Developmental** |
|---|---|---|
| **Your teacher's document** | HR completes **skills audits** of all staff and identifies **training needs**. Assists with **HR planning**. Information kept in an **employee database** | HR **plans and implements training** for staff — both **on the job and off the job** |
| The generic textbook version | Evaluating output to decide **pay, promotion, retention, termination** | Feedback used to **build skills** |

> **Use your teacher's version.** It's what your school marks against, and it also fits the exam answers better — the Knox trial's Q10 (a business *keeping data* on time spent online, meetings and client feedback) is **administrative** precisely because it is data collection for HR planning.
>
> **The tell:** administrative **records and audits**; developmental **plans and delivers**.

### 4.6 Rewards

> **Used to motivate employees and encourage them to work together as a team to achieve common goals.**

**Two axes:**

```
MONETARY  or  NON-MONETARY
INDIVIDUAL or  GROUP
```

**Designing a reward program — for the business:**

| Consideration |
|---|
| Business strategy |
| Economic conditions |
| Organisational objectives of rewards |
| Rewards and benefits offered by **competitors** |
| Relevant **awards and agreements**, minimum employment standards |
| **Union power** |
| **Profitability / viability** of the business |

**Designing a reward program — for the individual** *(often called **performance pay**: remuneration distributed according to individual employee performance)*

| Basis | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Performance related** | Incentive plans for performance **above standards or criteria**, bonuses, production-related criteria |
| **Job related** | Role and level of **responsibility**, scope of supervision, base pay, interpersonal skills, knowledge and skills, experience, **value to company** |
| **Other** | Group incentives |

> **Individual or team performance pay?** Both, depending on the nature of the work. **Team-based** pay encourages cooperation and communication toward common objectives; **individual** pay recognises outstanding contribution. Using both **prevents excessive competition** while still rewarding individual effort.

> **Why provide both monetary and non-monetary rewards?** Because **employees are motivated by different factors.** Monetary rewards — bonuses, commissions, pay increases — provide financial motivation. Non-monetary rewards — recognition, promotion, flexible working, career development — improve **job satisfaction and loyalty**. *McDonald\'s provides rewards and recognition programs, discounted meals and retail discounts, plus flexible working and career development opportunities.*

### 4.7 Global HR Strategies

> **Globalisation, technological development and HR applications have increased the complexity of managing HR.** High labour costs and skill shortages in Australia have led businesses to operate globally.

**Seven factors to consider before outsourcing globally:**

```
1. Labour costs                        5. Political stability
2. Service / product quality           6. Language and cultural differences
3. Infrastructure requirements         7. Skills and supply of labour overseas
4. Overseas labour market regulations
```

**The China example:** countries such as China have factories producing goods at a competitive price, so Australian companies have moved production there because it is cheaper.

| Impact | |
|---|---|
| ✅ | **Reduced costs** for the business |
| ❌ | **Loss of jobs and factory closures** in Australia |

**Australia's skills shortage** is major and will increase. Two strategies used to overcome it:

- The **Temporary Skills Shortage Visa**, allowing businesses to bring people from overseas to fill the shortage
- **Outsourcing functions overseas** to access people with those skills

### 4.8 Workplace Disputes

> **Disputes occur between employers and employees where their interests are in conflict.** Every business should have a **grievance procedure** to guide employees, employers and unions in resolving one.

**A grievance procedure policy:**

- Provides a **clear outline of the issues**
- Illustrates the **correct and appropriate processes** when raising a complaint
- Provides a mechanism for **quick resolution**

**The four resolution steps, in order:**

| # | Method | Who decides |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Negotiation** — discussion between both parties to resolve the dispute. Unions and employer associations may be involved | **The parties** |
| **2** | **Mediation** — an **independent party with no ties to either side** assists the parties to reach a settlement | **The parties** |
| **3** | **Conciliation** — **Fair Work Australia acts as a mediator and offers suggestions** to resolve the problem | **The parties** |
| **4** | **Arbitration** — the final stage. A **commissioner hears both cases and makes a decision that is legally binding** on both parties | **The commissioner** |

> **Confusing trio — Mediation vs Conciliation vs Arbitration**
> **Mediation** — the third party **facilitates only**. No suggestions.
> **Conciliation** — the third party **suggests solutions**. The parties still decide.
> **Arbitration** — the third party **imposes a binding decision**.
> Escalating intervention: facilitates → suggests → decides.

> **Overt vs covert industrial action:** **overt** action is visible and deliberate — strikes, work bans, work-to-rule (refusing tasks outside normal duties), lockouts, picket lines. **Covert** action is concealed — **absenteeism**, high turnover, sabotage, deliberate under-performance. Absenteeism appearing as an *indicator* in §6 and as *covert action* here is the same phenomenon read two ways.

### 4.9 Organisational Structures

| Structure | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flat** | Few management layers, **wide span of control** | Faster communication, empowerment | Manager overload, unclear reporting |
| **Tall** | Many management layers, **narrow span of control** | Clear career path, close supervision | Slow communication, bureaucracy |
| **Centralised** | Decisions made at the top | Consistency, control | Slow response, disempowerment |
| **Decentralised** | Decisions pushed to lower levels | Faster response, empowerment | Inconsistency, coordination challenges |
| **Functional** | Grouped by function | Specialisation, efficiency | Silos, poor cross-functional communication |
| **Matrix** | Dual reporting (function + project) | Flexible, cross-functional | Conflicting priorities, complex reporting |

> **Span of control** = the number of employees a manager directly supervises. Flat structures have a **wide** span; tall structures a **narrow** one.

## 5. Motivation Theories

### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

```
        Self-Actualisation
       (reach full potential)
      ────────────────────
         Esteem Needs
      (recognition, status)
     ────────────────────
      Social/Belonging Needs
       (teamwork, friendship)
      ────────────────────
          Safety Needs
    (job security, safe workplace)
   ────────────────────
      Physiological Needs
     (salary, basic conditions)
```

**Application**: Lower-level needs must be met before higher-level rewards will motivate. Pay and safety first, then belonging, then recognition, then growth opportunities.

### Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

| Factor | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hygiene factors** | Cause **dissatisfaction** if absent, but do **not motivate** when present | Salary, working conditions, job security, company policy, supervision |
| **Motivators** | Actually **motivate** and satisfy | Achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, personal growth |

**Application**: Fix hygiene factors to prevent dissatisfaction, **then** use motivators to engage employees. Raising pay alone removes dissatisfaction but does not create motivation.

### McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

| Theory X (authoritarian) | Theory Y (participative) |
|---|---|
| Workers are lazy and avoid work | Workers are self-motivated and enjoy work |
| Need close supervision and control | Need autonomy and responsibility |
| Motivated by punishment and reward | Motivated by intrinsic factors |
| Management style: command and control | Management style: empowerment and trust |

### Locke and Latham's Goal Setting Theory

Motivation increases when goals are **specific**, **challenging but achievable**, **accepted by the employee**, and accompanied by **feedback** on progress.

## 6. Effectiveness Indicators of Human Resource Management

> **An effective HR function is one where management and employees work well together in their efforts to achieve the goals of the business.** The effectiveness of that relationship is examined through seven indicators.

### 6.1 The Seven Indicators

| Indicator | Definition | What a poor result signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Corporate culture** | **The culture within an organisation — the values and beliefs within a business.** An effective culture is one that is *believed, demonstrated and acted upon* by both management and employees | A **positive** culture produces happier workers → lower turnover, higher productivity, better customer service, lower absenteeism. A **negative** culture produces less satisfied workers |
| **Benchmarking key variables** | **The process of measuring an employee's performance against established standards** | If employees repeatedly fail to meet performance goals, management must examine why. Employees are more likely to meet goals where there are effective **appraisal systems, training and development, and reward systems** |
| **Changes in staff turnover** | **The rate at which employees leave a business** | A high level caused by **voluntary separation** — mainly resignations — indicates **poor employment relations** |
| **Absenteeism** | **Employees being unable to attend work** for reasons such as illness or family responsibilities | Costly, because the work isn't done — it places additional demands on remaining staff and can cause **greater hostility**. A sharp rise over a short period signals unhappy, unsatisfied workers |
| **Level of disputation** | The number and severity of disputes in a workplace | **The more disputes, the less effective management is** at the employment relations function. Employers must examine why and rectify the practices causing them |
| **Accidents** | The **number of accidents per year** | Increases may be due to **lack of training**, or poor workplace health and safety procedures and policies |
| **Worker satisfaction** | **Whether employees are happy and contented and fulfilling their desires and needs at work** | Satisfied employees work more efficiently and value the organisation they work for |

> **Turnover isn't automatically bad** — some brings in fresh skills and removes poor performers. What matters is **who** is leaving, and whether the separation was **voluntary**. Losing high performers voluntarily is the warning sign.

### 6.2 Which Indicator Measures Which Strategy

> **Straight from your teacher's *Strategies and Indicators Summary*.** This is the table that answers "evaluate the effectiveness of [strategy]" — it tells you which evidence to cite.

| Strategy | Indicators used to measure it |
|---|---|
| **Leadership style** | Corporate culture · Benchmarks · Staff turnover · Absenteeism · Worker satisfaction |
| **Job design** | Staff turnover · Absenteeism · **Number of accidents** · Worker satisfaction |
| **Recruitment** | Corporate culture · Benchmarks · Staff turnover · Absenteeism |
| **Training and development** | Staff turnover · Absenteeism · **Number of accidents** · Corporate culture · Worker satisfaction |
| **Performance management** | Staff turnover · Absenteeism · Number of accidents · Worker satisfaction |
| **Rewards** | Staff turnover · Absenteeism · Number of accidents · Worker satisfaction |
| **Workplace disputes** | Staff turnover · Absenteeism · Worker satisfaction |

**The patterns worth noticing:**

- **Staff turnover and absenteeism measure everything.** If you can only remember two, remember these — they appear against all seven strategies.
- **Accidents** attach to the strategies that change *how work is done* — job design, training, performance management, rewards. Not to recruitment or leadership.
- **Corporate culture and benchmarks** attach to the strategies that shape *who is in the business and how they are led* — leadership, recruitment, training.
- **Workplace disputes is the narrowest** — three indicators, and notably **not** accidents or benchmarks.

> **How to use it in an answer:** *"The effectiveness of the training and development strategy can be measured through **changes in staff turnover** and the **number of accidents**. A reduction in both would indicate the strategy has improved employee capability and workplace safety…"*

## 7. Section IV & Short Answer Structures

### HR Strategy Answer Structure (4–6 marks)

1. **Define** the strategy — "Recruitment is the process of accumulating a pool of potential candidates…"
2. **Explain** how it works — describe the steps or methods
3. **Apply** to the business — link to a specific case study
4. **Evaluate** effectiveness — discuss advantages and disadvantages, impact on business performance

### Recommendation Structure (8 marks)

1. **Recommend** — "It is recommended that [business] implement [strategy]"
2. **Define** — explain what the strategy involves
3. **Reason 1** — how it addresses the identified problem
4. **Reason 2** — an additional benefit
5. **Link** — restate the recommendation with justification, tied to a business goal

### Assess / Evaluate the Effectiveness of HR Strategies

```
Intro:      Judgement + define HRM + name the strategies and case study
Body 1:     Strategy 1 → how it works → effect → EFFECTIVENESS INDICATOR
            (e.g. reduced staff turnover) → link to business goal
Body 2:     Strategy 2 → same structure → different indicator
Body 3:     Limitation / cost / counter-argument
Conclusion: Restate judgement, link to long-term goals
```

> **Top-band tip**: The difference between a mid-band and top-band HR answer is almost always the **effectiveness indicators** (§6). Quantify where you can: "staff turnover fell from 22% to 14%."

## 8. Common Exam Pitfalls

1. **Learn the HR cycle in order** — acquisition, development, maintenance, separation. Many questions are structured around one stage.
2. **Job description (the job) vs job specification (the person).**
3. **Redundancy (the job goes) vs dismissal (the person goes).**
4. **Voluntary vs involuntary separation** — and note that redundancy can be either.
5. **Award (industry safety net) vs enterprise agreement (workplace-negotiated, must pass BOOT).**
6. Don't just list strategies — link them to an **effectiveness indicator**.
7. **Induction is part of acquisition**, and is the bridge into development.
8. **Mediation / conciliation / arbitration** differ by how much power the third party has.
9. **Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators create satisfaction.** They are not the same.
10. Always show **interdependence** with operations, marketing and finance.

## 9. Glossary — Terms Students Mix Up

| Term | Plain meaning | Don't confuse with |
|---|---|---|
| **Job description** | Describes the **job's** tasks and responsibilities | **Job specification** — the person's required skills |
| **Job specification** | The skills, knowledge and abilities required | **Job description** — the duties |
| **Recruitment** | Accumulating a **pool** of candidates | **Selection** — choosing from the pool |
| **Selection** | Screening and choosing the best applicant | **Recruitment** — attracting them |
| **Induction** | Introducing a new employee to the business | **Training** — improving job performance |
| **Training** | Improving present and future job performance | **Development** — preparing for future roles |
| **Development** | Preparing employees for **future** responsibilities | **Training** — current role focus |
| **Maintenance** | Keeping staff motivated, loyal and retained | **Development** — growing their skills |
| **Redundancy** | The **job** no longer exists | **Dismissal** — the **person** is removed |
| **Summary dismissal** | Immediate dismissal for serious misconduct | **Unfair dismissal** — harsh, unjust or unreasonable |
| **Unfair dismissal** | Harsh, unjust or unreasonable dismissal | **Unlawful termination** — for a prohibited reason |
| **Constructive dismissal** | Employee resigns due to employer's conduct | Voluntary resignation |
| **Award** | Industry-wide minimum safety net | **Enterprise agreement** — workplace-negotiated |
| **Enterprise agreement** | Workplace agreement passing the BOOT | **Common law contract** — individual |
| **NES** | 11 legislated minimum entitlements for all employees | Awards (industry-specific) |
| **Employee** | Contract **of** service; entitled to leave and super | **Contractor** — contract **for** service |
| **Monetary benefit** | Cash or cash-equivalent from the business | **Non-monetary** — a non-cash benefit |
| **Intrinsic reward** | Satisfaction from **within** the person | Extrinsic rewards given by the business |
| **Fringe benefit** | Non-cash benefit forming part of remuneration (company car) | Intrinsic reward |
| **Piece rate** | Pay based on **output** produced | Commission (pay based on **sales**) |
| **Mediation** | Third party **facilitates**; parties decide | **Arbitration** — third party **decides** |
| **Conciliation** | Third party **suggests** solutions | **Mediation** — only facilitates |
| **Arbitration** | Third party imposes a **binding** decision | Mediation and conciliation (non-binding) |
| **Strike** | **Employees** stop work | **Lockout** — **employer** shuts them out |
| **Span of control** | Number of employees a manager supervises | Chain of command (reporting lines) |
| **Turnover** | Rate at which staff leave and are replaced | **Absenteeism** — unplanned days off |
| **Hygiene factors** | Prevent dissatisfaction; don't motivate | **Motivators** — actively create satisfaction |
| **EEO** | Decisions based on merit; barriers removed | Anti-discrimination law (prohibits conduct) |
| **Sham contracting** | Illegally treating an employee as a contractor | Legitimate use of contractors |
