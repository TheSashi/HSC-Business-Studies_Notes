## 1. Inputs

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformed resources** | The inputs **changed** by the process | **Materials** (raw materials, intermediate goods), **Information** (internal and external), **Customers** (where the customer themselves is changed, e.g. a haircut) |
| **Transforming resources** | The inputs that **carry out** the change | **Human resources** (skills, training, motivation), **Facilities** (plant, machinery, factory layout) |

> **Exam trap**: When a question mentions **customised products**, the key point is that **customers become a transformed resource** — they enter the process and shape it directly.

## 2. Transformation Processes — The Four Vs

| V | What it means | High | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | How much is produced | Efficient, automated, low unit cost, capital intensive | Manual, skilled labour, high unit cost |
| **Variety** | Range of products offered | Complex, flexible, expensive, process layout | Simple, efficient, standardised, product layout |
| **Variation in demand** | How much demand fluctuates | Need flexible resources, accurate forecasting, casual staff | Stable planning, consistent resource use |
| **Visibility** | Degree of customer contact | Skilled staff, responsive, quality interaction matters | Speed, consistency, cost control |

## 3. Technology, Task Design and Process Layout

### 3.1 Technology

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Leading edge** | The most advanced technology available — new, gives competitive advantage, but untested and expensive |
| **Established** | Proven technology that has been widely adopted — reliable and cheaper, but no advantage over competitors |

Benefits: faster production, fewer errors, consistent quality, long-term labour cost savings.

### 3.2 Task Design

**Task design**: The process of classifying job activities so employees can perform them easily.

Steps: define the job → analyse it into duties → allocate difficulty and time → match to awards → articulate through **job descriptors**.

### 3.3 Process Layout Types

| Type | Use | Characteristics | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Process (functional) layout** | High variety, low volume | Equipment grouped by **function**; flexible; slower | Hospital, bank, printery |
| **Product layout** | High volume, standardised | **Assembly line**; consistent quality; inflexible | Car factory, Foxconn |
| **Fixed position layout** | Large, immovable products | Workers and equipment come **to the site** | Construction, shipbuilding |
| **Office layout** | Information flow | Arranged for communication and workflow | Open-plan offices |

## 4. Monitoring, Control and Improvement

> ✅ **This is a PROCESS, not a strategy.** The syllabus lists *"monitoring, control and improvement"* as the fourth bullet under **transformation processes**, inside **Operations processes** — alongside the 4 Vs, sequencing and scheduling, and technology/task design/layout.
>
> ⚠️ **Why it feels like a strategy:** the words **control** and **improvement** appear a *second* time in the syllabus, under **Operations strategies** → *"quality management — control, assurance, improvement"*. Two different dot points, same two words. See the confusing pair below.

| Stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Monitoring** | Measuring **actual** performance against **planned** performance using KPIs |
| **Controlling** | Assessing KPIs against targets, **investigating variances**, and taking **corrective action** |
| **Improvement** | The systematic reduction of inefficiencies, waste and bottlenecks |

**Common operations KPIs**: lead times, inventory turnover, defect rates, capacity utilisation, unit costs, waste levels, warranty claims.

**Areas of improvement**: time, process flows, quality, cost, efficiency.

> **Continuous improvement** = an ongoing commitment to achieving perfection. Requires **staff involvement** at every level — it cannot be imposed from the top alone.

> **Confusing term — Bottleneck**: The point in a process with the **lowest capacity**, which limits the output of the *entire* system. Improving anything other than the bottleneck does not increase total output.

## 6. Sequencing and Scheduling

> **Definitions — learn these exact wordings**
> **Sequencing** refers to the **order** in which activities in the operations process occur.
> **Scheduling** refers to the **length of time** activities take within the operations process.

### 6.1 Gantt Charts

**Gantt chart**: outlines the activities that need to be performed, **the order** in which they should be performed, and **how long** each activity is expected to take.

```
Task          Week 1   Week 2   Week 3   Week 4   Week 5   Week 6
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Shop refit    ████████████████
Purchase      ─────────────────████████████
  stock
Staff         ─────────────────████████
  training
Marketing     ─────────────────────────████████████
Opening       ───────────────────────────────────────────█
```

**Advantages of Gantt charts:**

1. Show which activities can be performed **concurrently** (at the same time)
2. Identify **bottlenecks** and scheduling conflicts
3. Easy to communicate the plan to staff and stakeholders
4. Show total project duration at a glance
5. Allow progress to be tracked against plan

### 6.2 Critical Path Analysis (CPA)

**Critical path analysis**: shows **what needs to be done**, **how long tasks take**, and **what order** is necessary to complete those tasks.

> **The critical path is the shortest time to complete all of the tasks.**

> **How this actually works — read this once and it will never confuse you again.**
> A CPA diagram contains several possible **paths** from start to finish. You add up the durations along each path.
>
> - The **longest** path is the **critical path**.
> - Because every task must be finished, that longest path *is* the **shortest possible time** in which the whole project can be completed.
>
> So "longest path" and "shortest completion time" are **the same number** viewed two ways. The path is longest; the project time it produces is the shortest achievable. Both phrasings are correct — don't let them trip you up.

**Key CPA terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Critical path** | The sequence of activities that determines the total project duration |
| **Critical activity** | An activity **on** the critical path — it **cannot be delayed** without delaying the whole project |
| **Float (slack)** | The amount of time a non-critical activity **can** be delayed without delaying the project. Critical activities have **zero float** |
| **Concurrent activities** | Activities that can be carried out **at the same time** |
| **Dependency** | An activity that cannot start until another finishes |

**Worked example — Critical Path Analysis**

| Activity | Duration (days) | Must follow |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | — |
| B | 4 | — |
| C | 2 | A |
| D | 5 | B |
| E | 3 | C and D |

**Network:**

```
           A (3)        C (2)
     ┌──────────►○──────────►┐
 START                        ├──►  E (3)  ──► FINISH
     └──────────►○──────────►┘
           B (4)        D (5)
```

**Step 1 — list every path and total it:**

- Path 1: A → C → E = 3 + 2 + 3 = **8 days**
- Path 2: B → D → E = 4 + 5 + 3 = **12 days**

**Step 2 — identify the critical path:** the longest path is **B → D → E**.

**Step 3 — state the project duration:** the shortest time to complete all tasks is **12 days**.

**Step 4 — calculate float on the non-critical path:**
Path A→C→E takes 8 days but has 12 days available, so **A and C each have 4 days of float**. They can be delayed up to 4 days without delaying the opening.

**Answering the question "which task determines when E can be performed?"** → **D**, because E cannot begin until both C (finished day 5) and D (finished day 9) are complete. The later of the two — D — is the constraint.

**How CPA assists the transformation process:**

- Identifies which activities are **critical**, so management allocates resources there first
- Reveals **float**, allowing staff and equipment to be redeployed from non-critical tasks
- Reduces **lead time** by showing which activities can run **concurrently**
- Improves **dependability** by producing a realistic completion date
- Supports the **speed** performance objective

## ⚠️ Confusing pairs in this section

| Pair | The tell |
|---|---|
| **Monitoring, control and improvement** (process) vs **quality management: control, assurance, improvement** (strategy) | ⭐ **Both exist in the syllabus.** The *process* one is about **performance against plan** — measure, find the variance, fix it. The *strategy* one is about **defect prevention** — inspection, ISO 9000 systems, TQM. If the question is about KPIs and corrective action it's the **process**; if it's about quality standards and defects it's the **strategy** |
| **Transformed** vs **transforming resources** | Transformed resources are **changed**. Transforming resources **do the changing** |
| **Sequencing** vs **scheduling** | Sequencing = **order**. Scheduling = **duration** |
| **Gantt chart** vs **CPA** | Gantt shows activities against a **timeline** — best for *visualising* progress. CPA shows **dependencies** and what cannot be delayed — best for *managing* time and resources |
| **Critical path** vs **number of tasks** | The critical path is about **time**, not how many activities are on it |
| **Float** vs **critical activity** | Critical activities have **zero float** by definition |
| **Lead time** vs **cycle time** | Lead time = ordering to receiving. Cycle time = time to produce **one unit** |
| **Monitoring** vs **controlling** | Monitoring **measures**. Controlling **investigates the variance and acts** |
| **Process layout** vs **product layout** | Process = grouped by **function**, high variety. Product = **assembly line**, high volume |
