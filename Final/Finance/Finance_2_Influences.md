## 1. Internal Sources of Finance

Internal sources are recorded under **equity** in the balance sheet. The money comes from the owners or from the business's own activities.

| Source | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Owner's equity** | Funds contributed by owners or partners to establish and build the business |
| **Retained profits** | Net profit that has been **reinvested** in the business. Added as equity — increases the owner's claim |

> **Owner's Equity section of the Balance Sheet** = Capital **+** Retained Profits

---

## 2. External Sources of Finance

Financed from external organisations — either **debt** or **equity**.

- **Debt** = any money that has been **borrowed**. Short-term debt is repayable within 12 months; long-term is over 12 months.
- **Equity** = attached to the **ownership of shares** in an incorporated business — the owner's financial claim on the assets.

### 2.1 Debt Finance — Short Term

| Source | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Overdraft** | Flexibility to borrow from a bank at short notice through a cheque/current account. Interest must be paid. Used for **working capital** and claimable as a tax deduction. More flexible than a bank loan; assists with short-term **liquidity** issues |
| **Commercial bill** | A written order for a loan amount **guaranteed by the business's bank**. Borrowed from businesses with surplus funds. Normally **30–180 days**, usually for amounts **over $100,000** |
| **Factoring** | Selling **accounts receivable** to a specialist factoring firm to create cash inflow. The factoring company takes over management and collection of the unpaid accounts and pays the seller the value **minus a commission**. Business receives up to **90% within 48 hours**. The **riskiest** source because of unpaid debts — that risk is borne by the debt collection agency |

### 2.2 Debt Finance — Long Term

| Source | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mortgage** | A loan repaid over a set number of years with interest, used to purchase **land or property**. The property becomes the **security**. If the business defaults, the bank takes and sells the property |
| **Debenture** | Long-term debt acquired by offering a **prospectus** to the general public on the securities exchange. Set interest rate for a fixed time. **More risky to the lender than a mortgage** — not tied to specific property, so there is no security. Unlike shares, the level of profit does **not** affect the interest rate |
| **Unsecured notes** | Usually issued by finance companies to gain funds. **Higher interest rates** reflecting greater risk. Also called **bonds** |
| **Leasing** | Leasing non-current assets (cars, equipment) for payments to the owner. Reduces the cost of acquiring assets as full value need not be paid instantly. **Lease payments are tax deductible** — they appear under Expenses in the Profit and Loss Statement, reducing net profit and therefore company tax |

**Two types of lease:**

1. **Operating lease** — short periods, normally **shorter than the life** of the asset. The **owner** carries out maintenance.
2. **Financial lease** — the lessor purchases the asset on behalf of the lessee. Runs for the **lifetime of the asset**.

> **Why lease rather than buy?** Lease costs are tax deductible; flexibility if the asset is only needed for a short period; distributes the cost over time, which is better for **cash flow**.

### 2.3 Equity Financing

Equity financing comes from the **owners**. Equity reflects the owner's financial claim on the assets of the business.

| Legal structure | Term used |
|---|---|
| Sole trader / Partnership | **Owners' Equity** |
| Public / Private companies | **Shareholders' Funds** |

**Private equity** — current owners invite people or organisations to invest. Normally **proprietary companies** (Pty Ltd). Shareholders do not necessarily have control, and there is no guarantee of dividends.

**Public equity** — companies listed on the **ASX**; anyone can invest.

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Ordinary shares** | Issued on the ASX, with or without voting rights at the AGM |
| **New share issues** | Shares sold for the **first time** on the public market — either an **IPO** (initial public offering) or a new offering if more finance is needed |
| **Rights issue** | The company offers **existing shareholders** the chance to buy more shares |
| **Placements** | Additional shares offered to **specific institutions** (venture capitalists) and specific investors |
| **Share purchase plans** | An offer to existing shareholders to purchase more shares **without brokerage fees**, normally **below** normal prices. Requires **ASIC** permission |

| Equity — Advantages | Equity — Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No debt servicing costs such as interest | Takes **time** |
| If the business makes a loss, no dividend needs to be paid (though this may worry shareholders into selling) | **Dilutes ownership** |

### 2.4 Financial Grants

Financial **gifts** from government to help businesses establish. Strict criteria: location, personal characteristics of the owner, type of industry, type of goods/services. Criteria commonly include Indigenous people, regional businesses, the IT industry, and businesses providing innovative or essential services.

> The government is **not paid back** and gets **no ownership**. Its return comes through increased **employment, growth and taxes**.

## 3. Financial Institutions

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| **Banks** | Accept deposits and provide loans; the largest source of funds for business |
| **Investment banks** | Trade in money, securities and financial futures; arrange **long-term finance** for expansion; advise on **foreign exchange cover**, **mergers** (two businesses agree to combine, e.g. Westpac and St George) and **takeovers** (one business buys another, which ceases to exist); operate **unit trusts**; arrange **overseas finance** at cheaper interest rates. *Example: Macquarie Bank* |
| **Finance and life insurance companies** | Non-bank financial intermediaries specialising in **smaller commercial finance**. Provide secured and unsecured loans. Policyholders pay a **premium**, which is lent to businesses; the company earns income from interest |
| **Superannuation funds** | Provide funds to the corporate sector through investment of **superannuation contributions** |
| **Unit trusts** (mutual funds) | Take funds from a **large number of small investors** and invest them in specific types of financial assets |
| **Australian Securities Exchange (ASX)** | Market operator, clearing house and payment system facilitator. Oversees compliance with operating rules and promotes **corporate governance** among listed companies |

### 3.1 Primary vs Secondary Market — a guaranteed exam distinction

| | **Primary market** | **Secondary market** |
|---|---|---|
| What is traded | **New** shares, issued for the first time | **Pre-owned** (existing) securities |
| Who gets the money | **The company** receives the proceeds | The **selling shareholder** — the company receives **nothing** |
| Purpose | Raise **new capital** | Provide **liquidity** so investors can buy and sell easily |

> Because the secondary market provides liquidity, it makes investing in the primary market more attractive.

## 4. Influence of Government

| Influence | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Monetary policy** | The **RBA** assists the federal government to achieve low inflation and economic growth. Securities and loans are bought and sold to put pressure on **interest rates** and alter the economic cycle. This impacts **debt servicing costs** — if interest rates rise, loan repayment costs rise |
| **ASIC** (Australian Securities and Investments Commission) | Enforces and administers the **Corporations Act**; protects consumers in investments, life and general insurance, superannuation and banking (except lending). Aims to reduce **fraud and unfair practices**. Ensures companies adhere to the law, collects company information and makes it public |
| **Company taxation** | Companies pay **company tax on profits**, paid **before** dividends are distributed to shareholders |

> **Why company tax matters to financial management**: Tax reduces net profit available for distribution and reinvestment. It also makes **debt more attractive than equity**, because interest payments are generally **tax-deductible** expenses while dividends are not.

## 5. Global Market Influences

Financial risks in global markets are **greater** than domestic ones, but such risk is necessary for business strategy.

| Influence | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Interest rates** | Comparison of domestic interest rates to rates in other countries |
| **Availability of funds** | The **ease** with which a business can access funds on the international financial market |
| **Global economic outlook** | The **projected changes** to the level of economic growth throughout the world |

> **Why are these "uncontrollable"?** They are external macro-environmental factors existing independently of any one business. A single firm cannot change global interest rates or economic trends — it can only develop **internal strategies to mitigate** their impact.

> **Negative global outlook → funding**: When the outlook is negative, lenders become risk-averse and restrict lending because they fear non-repayment. Finance becomes harder to obtain, and where available, carries a **higher interest rate**.

## ⚠️ Confusing pairs in this section

| Pair | The tell |
|---|---|
| **Debenture** vs **mortgage** | A mortgage is **secured against property**. A debenture has **no security** — which is why it is riskier to the lender |
| **Primary** vs **secondary market** | Primary = **new** shares, the **company** gets the money. Secondary = existing shares, the company gets **nothing** |
| **Overdraft** vs **bank loan** | Overdraft is **flexible** short-term borrowing on a current account. A loan is a **fixed amount for a fixed term** |
| **Operating** vs **financial lease** | Operating = **short**, owner maintains it. Financial = runs for the **asset's whole life** |
| **Factoring** vs **leasing** | Factoring **sells your receivables** for cash. Leasing **rents an asset** |
| **Merger** vs **takeover** | Merger = both **agree** to combine. Takeover = one buys the other, which **ceases to exist** |
| **ASIC** vs **ACCC** | ASIC = companies, the Corporations Act, financial services. ACCC = competition and consumers |
| **Debt** vs **equity** | Debt must be **repaid** and interest is **tax deductible**. Equity **dilutes ownership** and dividends are **not** deductible |
