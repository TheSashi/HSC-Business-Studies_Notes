## 1. Role of Finance

**Definition**: Financial management is the planning, sourcing and controlling of financial resources to achieve business objectives. It involves deciding how much money is needed, where it comes from, and how it is used.

### 1.1 Strategic Role

- Finance is critical for achieving the **strategic plan** — provides financial resources for growth, operations and stability
- Financial managers must balance **risk and return** — higher returns often mean higher risk
- Effective financial management enables businesses to:
  - Meet short-term obligations (pay bills, wages, suppliers)
  - Invest in long-term growth (new equipment, expansion, R&D)
  - Provide returns to owners/shareholders (dividends, capital growth)

### 1.2 Financial Goals

| Goal Type | Timeframe | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | Short-term (within 12 months) | Ability to meet short-term debts as they fall due |
| **Profitability** | Short-term | Ability to generate profit from operations |
| **Efficiency** | Short-term | Maximising output from minimum input |
| **Growth** | Long-term (2–5 years) | Expansion of business operations, market share, assets |
| **Solvency** | Long-term | Ability to meet long-term debts and remain in business |

> **Key distinction**: Liquidity ≠ Solvency. A business can be liquid (pay short-term debts) but insolvent (cannot meet long-term obligations), or vice versa.

### 1.3 Interdependence with Other Functions

| Function | Relationship with Finance |
|---|---|
| **Operations** | Finance provides capital for production equipment, raw materials, technology. Operations generates costs that finance must manage. Budget constraints limit operational choices. |
| **Marketing** | Finance allocates budgets for advertising, promotions, market research. Marketing generates revenue that finance manages and distributes. |
| **Human Resources** | Finance covers wages, training costs, recruitment budgets, benefits. HR decisions (hiring, training) directly impact the financial position. |

> **Case Study — Apple**: Finance provides billions for R&D (operations) → new products like iPhone 16. Marketing budgets fund global campaigns. HR receives funds for talent acquisition in AI/engineering. Revenue from sales flows back to finance for reinvestment.

## 2. Sources of Finance

### 2.1 Internal Sources

| Source | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner's equity** | Personal funds invested by the owner(s) | Sole trader using savings to start a café |
| **Retained profits** | Profit kept in the business rather than distributed as dividends | Apple retains profits for R&D and acquisitions |
| **Sale of assets** | Selling unused equipment, property or investments to raise cash | Selling an old warehouse to fund new technology |
| **Tighter credit control** | Reducing the time customers take to pay (fewer debtor days) → more cash available sooner | Offering 2/10 net 30 terms |

### 2.2 External Debt (Short-Term)

| Source | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overdraft** | Bank allows account to go below zero up to a limit | Flexible, quick access, pay interest only on amount used | High interest rates, bank can recall at any time |
| **Trade credit** | Supplier allows business to pay for goods/services later (e.g. 30 days) | No interest, improves cash flow | Late payment may damage supplier relationship |
| **Hire purchase** | Asset purchased with deposit, balance paid in instalments + interest | Spread cost over time, use asset immediately | Total cost higher due to interest, don't own until final payment |
| **Bills of exchange** | Written promise to pay a specific amount on a specific date | Can be sold/discounted for immediate cash | Interest charges, legal document complexity |

### 2.3 External Debt (Long-Term)

| Source | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bank loans** | Lump sum borrowed, repaid over set period with interest | Fixed repayment schedule, predictable | Interest payments, collateral may be required |
| **Mortgages** | Loan secured against property (usually 15–30 years) | Lower interest rates (secured), long repayment | Property at risk if default, large total interest cost |
| **Debentures** | Long-term bonds issued to investors at fixed interest rate | Large amounts raised, fixed interest cost | Interest must be paid regardless of profit |
| **Leasing** | Renting an asset for a period (operating or finance lease) | No large upfront cost, tax-deductible payments | Don't own asset, ongoing costs, restrictions on use |

### 2.4 External Equity

| Source | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shares (IPO)** | Selling ownership stakes on the ASX | Large capital raised, no repayment needed | Dilutes ownership, shareholders expect dividends |
| **Venture capital** | Investment from firms for high-growth startups | Expertise + funding, no debt | Significant equity given up, investor influence |
| **Crowdfunding** | Raising small amounts from many people online | Marketing exposure, validates demand | Time-consuming, platform fees, no guaranteed amount |
| **Private equity** | Investment firms buy significant ownership stakes | Large capital injection, strategic guidance | Loss of control, pressure for high returns |

### 2.5 Debt vs Equity Comparison

<table>
<tr><th>Feature</th><th>Debt</th><th>Equity</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Repayment</b></td><td>Must be repaid with interest</td><td>No repayment obligation</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Ownership</b></td><td>No dilution of ownership</td><td>Dilutes ownership (new shareholders)</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Interest</b></td><td>Interest is tax-deductible</td><td>Dividends paid from after-tax profit</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Risk</b></td><td>Higher financial risk (must repay regardless of profit)</td><td>Lower financial risk (no fixed payments)</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Control</b></td><td>No loss of control</td><td>New shareholders may influence decisions</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Cost</b></td><td>Interest rate is fixed/known</td><td>Cost depends on dividends + capital growth expected</td></tr>
</table>

> **Apple**: Primarily uses equity (retained profits + shares) — avoids high debt levels. Strong cash reserves mean less reliance on external debt. This is a conservative, low-risk financial strategy.

### 2.6 Financial Institutions

| Institution | Role in Finance |
|---|---|
| **Investment Banks** (e.g. Macquarie) | Trade securities (shares, bonds), arrange long-term finance, advise on mergers/takeovers, manage unit trusts, arrange overseas finance |
| **Finance & Life Insurance Companies** | Non-bank intermediaries offering unsecured and secured loans to businesses and individuals |
| **Superannuation Funds** | Investment funds built from employer/employee contributions — invest in shares, property, bonds |
| **Unit Trusts / Mutual Funds** | Pool small investor funds into diversified portfolios managed by professionals |
| **ASX (Australian Securities Exchange)** | Primary market = new shares (IPOs); Secondary market = existing shares traded between investors |

## 3. Financial Statements

Financial statements provide a picture of a business's financial health. They are used by owners, managers, investors, creditors and government to make informed decisions.

### 3.1 Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)

**Purpose**: Shows the financial **position** of a business at a **point in time** (e.g. "as at 30 June 2025").

**Accounting Equation**:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
```

#### Components

<table>
<tr><th>Category</th><th>Sub-Category</th><th>Examples</th></tr>
<tr><td rowspan="2"><b>Assets</b><br>(what the business owns)</td><td>Current Assets</td><td>Cash, inventory, accounts receivable, prepayments</td></tr>
<tr><td>Non-Current Assets</td><td>Property, plant, equipment (PPE), vehicles, land, intangible assets (patents, goodwill)</td></tr>
<tr><td rowspan="2"><b>Liabilities</b><br>(what the business owes)</td><td>Current Liabilities</td><td>Accounts payable, short-term loans, accrued expenses, GST collected</td></tr>
<tr><td>Non-Current Liabilities</td><td>Long-term loans, mortgages, debentures, deferred tax</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Owner's Equity</b><br>(owner's claim on assets)</td><td>—</td><td>Contributed capital + Retained profits (accumulated profits not paid as dividends)</td></tr>
</table>

#### WORKED EXAMPLE — Balance Sheet

> **TechGadget Pty Ltd — Balance Sheet as at 30 June 2025**

| Equipment | 80,000 |
| Less: Accumulated depreciation | (25,000) |

| **LIABILITIES** | $ |
|---|---:|
| **Current Liabilities** | |
| Accounts payable | 22,000 |
| Short-term loan | 15,000 |
| GST collected | 8,000 |

| **Non-Current Liabilities** | |
| Mortgage | 250,000 |

#### Key Concepts

- **Historical cost concept**: Assets are recorded at their original purchase price (not current market value)
- **Capitalised expenses**: Purchase price + delivery + installation + training = asset value on balance sheet, then depreciated over useful life
- **Depreciation**: Systematic reduction in the value of a non-current asset over its useful life (wear and tear, age, obsolescence)
- **Appreciation**: Increase in value over time (typically land and property)

### 3.2 Profit and Loss Statement (Income Statement)

**Purpose**: Shows the financial **performance** of a business over a **period** (e.g. "for the year ended 30 June 2025").

**Formula**:

```
Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold = Gross Profit
Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = Net Profit (before tax)
Net Profit − Tax = Net Profit after Tax
```

#### WORKED EXAMPLE — Profit and Loss Statement

> **TechGadget Pty Ltd — Profit and Loss Statement for the year ended 30 June 2025**

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| **Sales Revenue** | 850,000 |
| Less: Cost of Goods Sold | (340,000) |
| **Gross Profit** | **510,000** |
| **Operating Expenses** | |
| Wages and salaries | (180,000) |
| Rent | (48,000) |
| Utilities | (12,000) |
| Insurance | (15,000) |
| Advertising | (25,000) |
| Depreciation | (18,000) |
| Interest on loans | (22,000) |
| *Total Operating Expenses* | *(320,000)* |
| **Net Profit before Tax** | **190,000** |
| Less: Income Tax (30%) | (57,000) |
| **Net Profit after Tax** | **133,000** |

### 3.3 Cash Flow Statement

**Purpose**: Shows actual **cash movements** into and out of the business over a **period**. Distinguishes between cash received and revenue earned.

**Three Sections**:

| Section | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Operating Activities** | Cash from day-to-day business (sales, receipts from customers, payments to suppliers/employees) |
| **Investing Activities** | Cash used for/acquired from long-term assets (buying/selling equipment, property, investments) |
| **Financing Activities** | Cash from/to owners and lenders (share issues, loan repayments, dividends paid) |

**Formula**:

```
Opening Cash Balance + Net Cash Flow = Closing Cash Balance
```

> **Critical distinction**: A profitable business can have poor cash flow. Example: a business makes $100,000 profit but customers haven't paid yet (credit sales recorded as revenue but no cash received). Meanwhile, the business must still pay rent, wages and suppliers in cash.

**Reasons for poor cash flow despite profitability**:
- High credit sales (revenue recorded before cash received)
- Unsold inventory (cash tied up in stock)
- Large capital purchases (cash outflow for assets)
- Seasonal fluctuations in sales

### 3.4 Limitations of Financial Statements

| Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Normalised earnings** | Financial results adjusted for cyclical ups and downs and one-off events (e.g. profit from selling land with capital gain removed to show true operating performance) |
| **Capitalised expenses** | Purchase price of non-current assets added to balance sheet (not expensed immediately), then written off as depreciation over useful life — can make a year look more profitable than it really is |
| **Valuing assets** | Historical cost may not reflect current market value. Inflation causes appreciation; wear and tear causes depreciation. Assets may be over- or under-valued. |
| **Subjective estimates** | Useful life of assets, bad debt provisions, and inventory valuations involve judgement — different accountants may produce different results |

## 4. Financial Ratios

Financial ratios are calculations that measure a business's financial performance, position and efficiency. They are used to compare performance over time (past trends) and against industry averages (competitors).

> **No ideal ratios exist in isolation** — always compare to previous periods and industry benchmarks.

### 4.1 Profitability Ratios

<table>
<tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th>What It Shows</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Gross Profit Ratio</b></td><td>(Gross Profit ÷ Sales) × 100</td><td>Efficiency of production/purchasing — how much profit remains after cost of goods sold</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Net Profit Ratio</b></td><td>(Net Profit ÷ Sales) × 100</td><td>Overall profitability after ALL expenses — true measure of business performance</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Return on Owners' Equity</b></td><td>(Net Profit ÷ Total Equity) × 100</td><td>Return generated for owners on their investment — key measure for shareholders</td></tr>
</table>

#### WORKED EXAMPLE — Profitability Ratios

> Using TechGadget Pty Ltd data (from P&L above):
> - Sales = $850,000 | Gross Profit = $510,000 | Net Profit after Tax = $133,000 | Total Equity = $345,000

```
Gross Profit Ratio = ($510,000 ÷ $850,000) × 100 = 60%
Net Profit Ratio = ($133,000 ÷ $850,000) × 100 = 15.6%
Return on Owners' Equity = ($133,000 ÷ $345,000) × 38.6%
```

**Interpretation**: TechGadget retains 60 cents of every dollar after production costs (strong). 15.6 cents of every dollar becomes profit after all expenses. Owners earn 38.6% return on their equity — a strong return compared to bank interest rates (~4-5%).

**Strategies to improve profitability**:
- **Cost controls**: Bulk buying (economies of scale), outsourcing non-core activities, establishing cost centres
- **Revenue controls**: Marketing mix adjustments, product differentiation, pricing strategies

### 4.2 Liquidity Ratios

<table>
<tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th>Ideal</th><th>What It Shows</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Current Ratio</b></td><td>Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities</td><td>2:1</td><td>Ability to pay short-term debts — for every $1 of current debt, $2 of current assets</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Quick Ratio</b></td><td>(Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities</td><td>1:1</td><td>Ability to pay short-term debts WITHOUT selling inventory — more conservative test</td></tr>
</table>

#### WORKED EXAMPLE — Liquidity Ratios

> Using TechGadget data: Current Assets = $105,000 | Inventory = $28,000 | Current Liabilities = $45,000

```
Current Ratio = $105,000 ÷ $45,000 = 2.33:1
Quick Ratio = ($105,000 − $28,000) ÷ $45,000 = $77,000 ÷ $45,000 = 1.71:1
```

**Interpretation**: Current ratio of 2.33:1 exceeds the ideal 2:1 — TechGadget has strong short-term liquidity. Quick ratio of 1.71:1 exceeds ideal 1:1 — even without selling inventory, the business can comfortably meet short-term obligations.

> **McDonald's Case Study**: Current ratio worsened from 1.43:1 (2022) to 1.16:1 (2023) — below ideal, but still adequate for a business with strong cash flows from daily transactions.

**Strategies to improve liquidity**:
- Control over current assets (collect receivables faster, reduce inventory levels)
- Control over current liabilities (negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers)
- Leasing instead of buying (preserves cash, spreads payments)
- Sale and leaseback (sell assets, lease them back — frees up cash)

### 4.3 Efficiency Ratios

<table>
<tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th>What It Shows</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Expense Ratio</b></td><td>(Total Expenses ÷ Sales) × 100</td><td>How efficiently the business controls costs — lowest possible ratio is best</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Accounts Receivable Turnover</b></td><td>Sales ÷ Accounts Receivable</td><td>How many times per year receivables are collected — then 365 ÷ answer = average debtor days</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Inventory Turnover</b></td><td>Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory</td><td>How many times inventory is sold and replaced per year</td></tr>
</table>

#### WORKED EXAMPLE — Efficiency Ratios

> TechGadget: Sales = $850,000 | Accounts Receivable = $32,000 | COGS = $340,000 | Inventory = $28,000 | Total Expenses = $320,000

```
Expense Ratio = ($320,000 ÷ $850,000) × 100 = 37.6%

Receivable Turnover = $850,000 ÷ $32,000 = 26.6 times per year
Debtor Days = 365 ÷ 26.6 = 13.7 days

Inventory Turnover = $340,000 ÷ $28,000 = 12.1 times per year
```

**Interpretation**: Debtor days of ~14 days is excellent (ideal is 30 days) — TechGadget collects cash quickly. Inventory turns over 12 times per year (~30 days) — efficient stock management.

**Strategies to improve efficiency**:
- **Factoring**: Sell accounts receivable to a debt collection agency for immediate cash (at a discount)
- **Discounts for early payment**: E.g. "2/10 net 30" — 2% discount if paid within 10 days, full amount due in 30 days
- **Tighter credit policies**: Reduce credit terms, conduct credit checks on new customers
- **Just-in-Time (JIT)**: Reduce inventory holding costs by ordering stock only when needed

### 4.4 Gearing (Solvency) Ratios

<table>
<tr><th>Ratio</th><th>Formula</th><th>Ideal</th><th>What It Shows</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Gearing Ratio</b></td><td>Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity</td><td>0.5:1 to 1:1</td><td>Proportion of debt vs equity — measures financial risk and reliance on borrowed funds</td></tr>
</table>

#### WORKED EXAMPLE — Gearing Ratio

> TechGadget: Total Liabilities = $295,000 | Total Equity = $345,000

```
Gearing Ratio = $295,000 ÷ $345,000 = 0.86:1
```

**Interpretation**: Gearing of 0.86:1 falls within the ideal range (0.5:1 to 1:1). TechGadget has a balanced mix of debt and equity — moderate financial risk.

- **Below 0.5:1** = Low gearing — business is under-leveraged, may not be using debt to maximise returns
- **Above 1:1** = High gearing — business relies heavily on debt, higher risk of insolvency if profits fall

**Important**: There are no strategies to directly "improve" gearing — the business must choose the best source of financing (debt vs equity) based on its circumstances.

### 4.5 How to Compare Ratios

| Method | Description | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Past Trends** | Compare ratios to previous years | If ratios are worsening → investigate causes and change strategies |
| **Industry Average** | Compare to competitors in the same industry | Shows how the business performs relative to the market |

#### McDonald's Example — Ratio Analysis

| Ratio | 2022 | 2023 | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 1.43:1 | 1.16:1 | ↓ Worsened (below ideal) |
| Net Profit Ratio | 27% | 33% | ↑ Improved |

> **McDonald's NP ratio improved** from 27% to 33% while **Yum Brands** (KFC, Pizza Hut) went from 19% to 23% — both improved, but McDonald's maintains stronger profitability. However, the declining current ratio suggests McDonald's is using more short-term debt to fund operations — a potential concern if cash flows weaken.

## 5. Conflict Between Financial Objectives

Financial goals often compete with each other — improving one can worsen another. Management must find the right balance.

### 5.1 Profitability vs Growth

| Goal | Impact on the Other |
|---|---|
| **Growth** requires spending money (new locations, staff, inventory, marketing) | Reduces short-term profit because costs increase before revenue catches up |
| **Profitability** focuses on maximising returns | May require limiting expansion, keeping operations lean |

> **Apple**: Aggressive growth into China and India requires massive upfront investment (stores, supply chains, marketing) — reduces short-term profitability but positions for long-term revenue growth. R&D spending ($30B+ annually) reduces current profit but ensures future product competitiveness.

### 5.2 Liquidity vs Profitability

| Goal | Impact on the Other |
|---|---|
| **Liquidity** means keeping cash available | Cash sitting in the bank earns low returns — reduces profitability |
| **Profitability** means investing cash for higher returns | Ties up cash in assets/investments — reduces liquidity and may create short-term cash shortages |

> **McDonald's**: Holds substantial cash reserves for liquidity (ability to respond to opportunities/threats). However, this cash could generate higher returns if invested in new restaurants or technology. The balance ensures McDonald's can weather economic downturns while still growing.

### 5.3 Short-Term vs Long-Term

| Goal | Impact on the Other |
|---|---|
| **Short-term profit** (cut costs now) | Cutting R&D, training, or maintenance improves current profit but harms future competitiveness |
| **Long-term sustainability** (invest for the future) | Requires spending now which reduces short-term profit |

> **BYD**: Heavy investment in battery technology and EV production reduces short-term profit. However, this positions BYD as a global EV leader — the world's largest EV manufacturer by 2024. Short-term sacrifice → long-term dominance.

## 6. Cash Flow Management

**Cash flow** = the movement of money in and out of a business over a period of time.

### 6.1 Cash Flow Statement vs Cash Flow Budget

| Document | Purpose | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Record of actual past cash movements | Historical (what happened) |
| **Cash Flow Budget** | Forecast of expected future cash movements | Future (what will happen) |

### 6.2 Importance of Cash Flow

- A profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash
- Cash is needed to pay wages, rent, suppliers, loan repayments
- Poor cash flow can force a business into insolvency even if it is profitable on paper

> **Key insight**: Profit ≠ Cash. A business may record a $100,000 sale on credit (revenue) but receive no cash for 60 days. Meanwhile, it must pay $50,000 in wages and $30,000 in rent THIS WEEK.

### 6.3 Cash Flow Cycle

```
Cash → Inventory → Production → Sales → Debtors → Cash
 |                                                          |
 +──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────+
                    (longer cycle = more working capital needed)
```

**The longer the cash flow cycle, the more working capital is required to fund day-to-day operations.**

### 6.4 Reasons for Cash Flow Problems

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High credit sales | Revenue recorded but cash not yet received |
| Unsold inventory | Cash tied up in stock sitting in warehouse |
| Negative cash flow | More cash going out than coming in (e.g. expansion period) |
| Seasonal fluctuations | Some businesses have peak and quiet periods |
| Unexpected expenses | Equipment breakdown, legal costs, natural disaster |
| Overtrading | Growing too fast — can't fund increased operations |

### 6.5 Strategies to Improve Cash Inflows

| Strategy | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Distribution of payments** | Spread large expenses over the year (e.g. quarterly insurance instead of annual lump sum) |
| **Prepay expenses** | Pay in advance for discounts — reduces future outflows |
| **Discounts for early payment** | Offer 2/10 net 30 — incentivises customers to pay faster |
| **Factoring** | Sell accounts receivable to a debt collection agency for immediate cash (at a small discount) |
| **Tighten credit terms** | Reduce debtor days — from 60 to 30 days |
| **Sell unused assets** | Convert idle equipment/property into cash |
| **Increase sales revenue** | More sales = more cash (especially cash/credit card sales) |

### 6.6 Strategies to Decrease Cash Outflows

| Strategy | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Negotiate longer payment terms** | Ask suppliers for 60 or 90 days instead of 30 — keeps cash in the business longer |
| **Reduce inventory (JIT)** | Just-in-Time ordering reduces cash tied up in stock |
| **Cut discretionary expenses** | Reduce non-essential spending (travel, entertainment, unused subscriptions) |
| **Lease instead of buy** | Spreads cost over time, preserves cash for operations |
| **Refinance at lower rates** | Replace high-interest loans with lower-interest alternatives |
| **Renegotiate contracts** | Seek better terms with landlords, suppliers, service providers |

### 6.7 Worked Example — Cash Flow Improvement

> **Problem**: A café has $15,000 cash in bank, $25,000 in accounts receivable (customers owe money), $20,000 in inventory, and $30,000 in accounts payable (bills due). The owner is concerned about making payroll next week ($8,000).

**Immediate actions**:
1. **Factor $15,000 of receivables** at 5% discount → receive $14,250 cash immediately
2. **Sell $5,000 of slow-moving inventory** at cost → receive $5,000 cash
3. **Negotiate 30-day extension** on $15,000 of payables → delays $15,000 outflow

**Result**: Cash position improves from $15,000 to $34,250 (immediate) — more than enough for payroll ($8,000) and other immediate obligations.

## 7. Working Capital Management

**Working Capital** = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

It represents the funds available for day-to-day operations. Positive working capital means the business can meet short-term obligations; negative working capital signals potential financial trouble.

### 7.1 Components of Working Capital

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Cash** | Money on hand and in bank accounts — most liquid asset |
| **Inventory** | Raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods waiting to be sold |
| **Debtors (Accounts Receivable)** | Money owed to the business by customers who purchased on credit |
| **Creditors (Accounts Payable)** | Money the business owes to suppliers for goods/services purchased on credit |

### 7.2 Working Capital Cycle

```
Working Capital Cycle = Inventory Holding Period + Debtor Collection Period − Creditor Payment Period
```

#### WORKED EXAMPLE

> **Data**: Inventory held for 60 days | Customers pay in 30 days | Suppliers given 45 days to pay

```
Working Capital Cycle = 60 + 30 − 45 = 45 days
```

**Interpretation**: The business needs to fund 45 days of operations from its own cash before receiving money from customers and after paying suppliers. A shorter cycle = less working capital needed = better cash flow.

#### Worked Example — Improving the Cycle

> If the business implements JIT to reduce inventory holding to 40 days, offers early payment discounts to reduce debtor days to 20 days, and negotiates 60-day payment terms with suppliers:

```
New Cycle = 40 + 20 − 60 = 0 days
```

**Interpretation**: The cycle is now zero — the business receives cash from customers before it needs to pay suppliers. This is the ideal scenario (common in supermarkets like Woolworths and Coles).

### 7.3 Strategies to Manage Working Capital

| Strategy | Impact on Cycle |
|---|---|
| **JIT (Just-in-Time)** | Reduces inventory holding period → shortens cycle |
| **Early payment discounts** | Reduces debtor collection period → shortens cycle |
| **Longer supplier terms** | Increases creditor payment period → shortens cycle |
| **Factoring** | Converts receivables to cash immediately → shortens cycle |
| **Cash pooling** | Consolidates cash from multiple accounts → improves cash availability |
| **Rolling budgets** | Continuously updated forecasts → better cash planning |

> **McDonald's**: Operates with a very short (often negative) working capital cycle. Customers pay immediately (cash/card), while McDonald's negotiates extended terms with suppliers. This generates significant free cash flow for expansion and shareholder returns.

## 8. Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis determines the point at which total revenue equals total costs — the business makes neither profit nor loss.

### 8.1 Key Formulas

| Formula | Calculation |
|---|---|
| **Total Revenue** | Price per unit × Quantity sold |
| **Total Costs** | Fixed Costs + (Variable Cost per unit × Quantity) |
| **Break-Even Point (units)** | Fixed Costs ÷ (Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit) |
| **Break-Even Point ($)** | Break-Even Units × Selling Price |
| **Contribution per unit** | Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit |
| **Margin of Safety** | Actual Sales − Break-Even Sales |
| **Target Profit (units)** | (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per unit |

### 8.2 WORKED EXAMPLE — Café

> **Data**: Fixed costs = $60,000/year | Selling price per coffee = $5 | Variable cost per coffee = $2

```
Contribution per unit = $5 − $2 = $3

BEP (units) = $60,000 ÷ $3 = 20,000 coffees

BEP ($) = 20,000 × $5 = $100,000
```

**Interpretation**: The café must sell 20,000 coffees (or $100,000 worth) per year to break even. Every coffee sold AFTER the 20,000th generates $3 profit.

#### Target Profit Calculation

> **Target**: Owner wants $30,000 profit

```
Target Profit (units) = ($60,000 + $30,000) ÷ $3 = 30,000 coffees
Target Profit ($) = 30,000 × $5 = $150,000
```

#### Margin of Safety

> If the café expects to sell 25,000 coffees:

```
Margin of Safety = 25,000 − 20,000 = 5,000 coffees (20%)
```

**Interpretation**: Sales can drop by 5,000 coffees (20%) before the business makes a loss. A higher margin of safety = lower risk.

### 8.3 Break-Even Chart

```
Revenue/Costs ($)
        |                              /  Total Revenue
        |                            /
        |                          /
  $100K |· · · · · · · · · · · ·X· · · · · · ·  ← Break-Even Point
        |                      /|
        |                    /  |
        |         Total   /    |  ← Profit Zone
        |        Costs  /     |
        |            /        |
        |          /          |
        |        /            |
  $60K  |──────/──────────────  ← Fixed Costs (horizontal line)
        |    /                |
        |  /                  |
        |/ ← Loss Zone        |
        +──────────────────────────────────── Quantity (coffees)
               20,000
```

**Reading the chart**:
- **Fixed Costs line**: Horizontal (costs don't change with quantity)
- **Total Costs line**: Starts at fixed costs, rises with each unit produced
- **Revenue line**: Starts at zero, rises with each unit sold
- **Break-Even Point**: Where revenue line crosses total costs line
- **Loss zone**: Left of break-even (costs > revenue)
- **Profit zone**: Right of break-even (revenue > costs)

### 8.4 Effects of Changes on Break-Even Point

| Change | Effect on BEP | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| ↑ Selling Price | BEP **decreases** | Each unit contributes more to covering fixed costs |
| ↑ Variable Costs | BEP **increases** | Each unit contributes less to covering fixed costs |
| ↑ Fixed Costs | BEP **increases** | More units needed to cover higher fixed costs |
| ↓ Selling Price | BEP **increases** | Each unit contributes less |
| ↓ Variable Costs | BEP **decreases** | Each unit contributes more |
| ↓ Fixed Costs | BEP **decreases** | Fewer units needed |

> **BYD**: Invested heavily in EV production (↑ fixed costs → ↑ BEP). However, economies of scale and battery cost reductions (↓ variable costs) lowered BEP over time, making EVs profitable at higher volumes.

## 9. Budgeting

A **budget** is a financial plan that sets out expected income and expenditure over a specific period. It is a tool for planning, coordination, control and communication.

### 9.1 Purpose of Budgeting

| Purpose | Description |
|---|---|
| **Planning** | Sets financial targets and allocates resources to achieve objectives |
| **Coordination** | Ensures all departments work towards the same financial goals |
| **Control** | Allows comparison of actual vs budgeted performance |
| **Communication** | Clearly communicates financial expectations to managers and staff |

### 9.2 Variance Analysis

**Variance** = Actual Result − Budgeted Amount

<table>
<tr><th>Category</th><th>Favourable (F)</th><th>Adverse/Unfavourable (A)</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Revenue</b></td><td>Actual > Budget (earned more than expected)</td><td>Actual < Budget (earned less than expected)</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Costs</b></td><td>Actual < Budget (spent less than expected)</td><td>Actual > Budget (spent more than expected)</td></tr>
</table>

> **Key rule**: For revenue, higher is better. For costs, lower is better. The same numerical difference can be Favourable or Adverse depending on whether it relates to income or expenditure.

#### Worked Example — Variance Analysis

| Item | Budget | Actual | Variance | F/A |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Sales Revenue | $500,000 | $520,000 | +$20,000 | F |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $200,000 | $215,000 | +$15,000 | A |
| Wages | $120,000 | $115,000 | −$5,000 | F |
| Rent | $48,000 | $48,000 | $0 | — |
| Advertising | $25,000 | $30,000 | +$5,000 | A |

**Interpretation**: Sales exceeded budget by $20,000 (Favourable) — strong performance. However, COGS was $15,000 over budget (Adverse) — production costs were higher than planned. Wages came in under budget (Favourable) — efficient staffing. Advertising overspend (Adverse) — need to investigate if the extra spending generated additional revenue.

### 9.3 Types of Budgets

| Budget Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Master Budget** | Overall financial plan combining all individual budgets |
| **Cash Budget** | Forecast of cash inflows and outflows |
| **Sales Budget** | Expected sales revenue and quantities |
| **Expense Budget** | Expected operating expenses by category |
| **Capital Expenditure Budget** | Planned spending on non-current assets |

## 10. Profitability Strategies

**Profit** = Total Revenue − Total Costs = (Price × Quantity) − (Fixed Costs + Variable Costs)

To increase profit, a business must either **increase revenue** or **decrease costs** (or both).

### 10.1 Cost Control Strategies

| Strategy | How It Increases Profit |
|---|---|
| **Economies of scale** | As production increases, average cost per unit decreases (bulk buying discounts, spreading fixed costs) |
| **Lean production** | Eliminate waste in all forms (overproduction, waiting, transport, defects, excess inventory) |
| **Outsource non-core activities** | Contract specialist firms for functions like IT, payroll, cleaning — often cheaper than doing in-house |
| **Negotiate supplier discounts** | Bulk orders, long-term contracts, early payment discounts reduce input costs |
| **Reduce overheads** | Renegotiate rent, switch to energy-efficient equipment, reduce unnecessary expenses |
| **Technology/automation** | Machinery and software reduce labour costs and increase output |
| **JIT (Just-in-Time)** | Order stock only when needed — reduces storage costs, waste, and obsolescence |

> **McDonald's**: Uses economies of scale (buys ingredients globally at lowest prices), lean production (standardised processes, minimal waste), and technology (self-service kiosks reduce labour costs) to maintain industry-leading profit margins.

### 10.2 Revenue Control Strategies

| Strategy | How It Increases Profit |
|---|---|
| **Product differentiation** | Unique features justify higher prices (premium pricing) |
| **Market development** | Enter new markets (geographic expansion, new demographics) to increase sales volume |
| **Product development** | Create new products for existing customers — increases revenue per customer |
| **Diversification** | New products for new markets — spreads risk, creates multiple revenue streams |
| **Price skimming** | High initial price on new products, gradually lowering — maximises early revenue |
| **Penetration pricing** | Low initial price to gain market share quickly —牺牲 short-term profit for volume |

> **Apple**: Revenue strategies include product differentiation (premium design/quality), price skimming (new iPhones launch at premium prices), product development (Apple Watch, AirPods, services like Apple Music), and market development (expanding in India and Southeast Asia).

> **BYD**: Uses penetration pricing in international markets to gain market share rapidly, combined with product differentiation through innovative battery technology.

## 11. Global Financial Strategies

### 11.1 Exchange Rates

An **exchange rate** is the value of one country's currency expressed in another country's currency.

| Scenario | Effect on Business |
|---|---|
| **AUD depreciation against importing country** | Costs MORE for Australian business (imports become more expensive in AUD) |
| **AUD appreciation against borrowing country** | Business REPAYS LESS (foreign debt costs less in AUD) |
| **AUD depreciation against lending country** | Business RECEIVES LESS (repayments worth less in AUD) |
| **AUD appreciation against exporting country** | Costs LESS (foreign inputs cheaper in AUD) |

> **Example**: If AUD falls from 0.70 USD to 0.65 USD:
> - An Australian importer buying US$100,000 of goods pays AUD $142,857 → AUD $153,846 (costs MORE)
> - An Australian exporter receiving US$100,000 gets AUD $142,857 → AUD $153,846 (receives MORE)

### 11.2 Interest Rates

- Businesses may borrow overseas where interest rates are lower
| Advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Lower interest rates** | Foreign rates may be significantly lower than Australian rates |
| **Fewer regulations** | Some foreign markets have less restrictive lending criteria |
| **Quicker access** | International capital markets may provide faster funding |

> **Risk**: Currency fluctuations can increase the effective cost of overseas borrowing if AUD depreciates.

### 11.3 Methods of International Payment

| Method | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Payment in advance** | Buyer pays before goods are shipped — safest for seller, riskiest for buyer |
| **Letter of credit** | Bank guarantees payment to seller once shipping documents are verified — reduces risk for both parties |
| **Bill of exchange** | Written promise to pay a specified amount on a specified date — can be traded/discounted |

### 11.4 Hedging and Derivatives

- **Hedging**: Reducing or eliminating the risk of adverse currency movements
- **Derivatives**: Financial instruments used to manage foreign exchange risk

| Derivative | Description |
|---|---|
| **Forward contracts** | Agree today to exchange currency at a fixed rate on a future date — locks in the rate |
| **Options** | Right (not obligation) to exchange currency at a fixed rate — protects against adverse movements while allowing benefit from favourable ones |
| **Futures** | Standardised contracts traded on an exchange to exchange currency at a set rate on a future date |

> **BYD**: When exporting EVs to Europe and Australia, BYD uses hedging strategies to manage currency risk — ensuring predictable costs and revenues despite exchange rate fluctuations.

## 12. Government Influence on Finance

### 12.1 Fiscal Policy

- The government budget showing **revenue sources (taxes)** and **spending**
- Used to expand or contract economic activity
- **Examples**: Introduction of the GST (10% on goods and services), changes to income tax rates, company tax rates
- Higher taxes → less disposable income for consumers → reduced spending → affects business revenue

### 12.2 Monetary Policy

- Government through the **Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA)** uses **interest rates** to control money supply and inflation
- RBA adjusts the **cash rate** → affects bank interest rates → encourages or discourages consumer spending and business investment

| RBA Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Increase** cash rate | Borrowing becomes more expensive → less spending → cools inflation |
| **Decrease** cash rate | Borrowing becomes cheaper → more spending → stimulates economy |

> **Impact on businesses**: Higher interest rates increase loan repayments (costs ↑), reduce consumer spending (revenue ↓), and increase the break-even point.

## 13. Limitations and Ethics in Finance

### 13.1 Limitations of Financial Statements

| Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Complexity** | Reports can be complicated for those without financial backgrounds |
| **Historical data** | Financial statements show past performance, not future |
| **Subjective judgements** | Estimates and assumptions affect reported figures |
| **Different accounting methods** | Businesses may use different depreciation methods, making comparisons difficult |

### 13.2 Normalised Earnings

- Earnings adjusted for **cyclical ups and downs** of the economy
- One-off events removed (e.g., sale of land with a capital gain)
- Show a **true reflection** of earnings from ongoing operations
- Found on Profit and Loss Statement and Retained Profit on Balance Sheet

### 13.3 Capitalised Expenses

- Purchase price of non-current assets is added to setup costs and recorded as the asset value on the balance sheet
- Then written off as depreciation over the useful life

**Worked Example:**

| Cost Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | $1,000,000 |
| Delivery costs | $500 |
| Installation costs | $1,500 |
| Training costs | $5,000 |
| **Value on Balance Sheet** | **$1,007,000** |

### 13.4 Valuing Assets

- **Historical cost concept**: Assets recorded at purchase price
- In reality, asset values change over time:
  - **Appreciation**: Land and property tend to increase in value → should be revalued to show true reflection
  - **Depreciation**: Equipment loses value over time → value should be reduced annually

### 13.5 Ethical Issues in Finance

| Issue | Description |
|---|---|
| **Creative accounting** | Manipulating financial records to present a better picture than reality |
| **Earnings management** | Timing revenue/expenses to smooth earnings |
| **Insider trading** | Using confidential information for personal gain |
| **Misleading reports** | Presenting financial data in a way that deceives stakeholders |
| **Related party transactions** | Unfair deals between the business and connected parties |

## 14. HSC Exam Response Structures for Finance

### 14.1 Formula for 2-Mark "Identify" Questions

```
Identify the ratio/concept + State what it measures or its formula
```

**Example**: "Identify one profitability ratio."
> The net profit margin ratio measures profitability by comparing net profit to sales revenue. Formula: (Net Profit ÷ Sales Revenue) × 100.

### 14.2 Formula for 3-Mark "Explain" Questions

```
State the concept + Explain how it works + Provide a business example
```

**Example**: "Explain how a business could improve its cash flow."
> One strategy to improve cash flow is to tighten credit terms (CONCEPT). This involves reducing the number of days customers have to pay, such as changing from 60 days to 30 days (EXPLANATION). For example, a retail clothing store could offer a 5% discount for payment within 7 days to encourage faster debtor collection (EXAMPLE).

### 14.3 Formula for 4-Mark "Discuss" / "Analyse" Questions

```
State the strategy/technique + Explain the positive impact + Explain a potential negative/limitation + Link back to the business context
```

### 14.4 Formula for 8-Mark "Evaluate" Questions

```
Introduction: Define the key term, state the judgement/position
Body Paragraph 1: Point + Evidence/Example + Link to question
Body Paragraph 2: Point + Evidence/Example + Link to question
Body Paragraph 3: Counterargument OR another factor + Link
Conclusion: Restate judgement with a qualifying statement
```

### 14.5 PEEL Structure for All Responses

| Letter | Meaning | What to Write |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Point | State your main idea or argument |
| **E** | Explain | Show how/why — cause and effect reasoning |
| **E** | Evidence | Use a case study, statistic, or example |
| **L** | Link | Connect back to the question and business goals |

### 14.6 Common Finance Question Verbs

| Verb | What to Do | Marks Guide |
|---|---|---|
| **Identify** | Name/state a concept | 1–2 marks |
| **Describe** | Give features/characteristics | 2–3 marks |
| **Explain** | How/why — show understanding with example | 3–4 marks |
| **Analyse** | Break down + examine relationships | 4–5 marks |
| **Discuss** | Present multiple perspectives | 5–6 marks |
| **Evaluate** | Make a judgement with supporting arguments | 7–8 marks |
| **Justify** | Support a position with evidence | 6–8 marks |

### 14.7 Finance Essay Template (8 marks)

```
[SENTENCE 1 — Define key term from question]
[SENTENCE 2 — State your overall position/thesis]

BODY 1: First reason the strategy is effective
- Identify strategy
- Explain how it works (use ratios/numbers if possible)
- Link to improving business performance

BODY 2: Second reason OR a counterargument
- Identify another strategy or limitation
- Explain with evidence/example
- Acknowledge the complexity

CONCLUSION:
"Ultimately, [strategy] is effective in [context] because... 
However, its success depends on [qualifying factor]. 
Therefore, a balanced approach combining [strategies] would 
be most beneficial for the business."
```
